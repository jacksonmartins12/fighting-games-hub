A Basic Game Plan

Many beginner wins come from the opponent's mistakes rather than from a plan. gootecks' advice is to build your plan around one clear goal and work backwards from it: push the opponent into the corner, where they can't walk back, their options shrink and most players crack under pressure. Ryu's plan has barely changed since Street Fighter II, and it works as a template for almost every character: pressure safely with fireballs and pokes, and anti-air whoever jumps.

StepWhat to doExample tools (Ryu)

2. Score a knockdownA knockdown gives you time to walk forward and start your offense.Sweep (2HK) as a whiff punish, throws, combos that end in a knockdown

4. Mix up on wakeupStrike, throw, shimmy, and a low or an overhead. Watch whether they tech, block or press a button, and adjust.Meaty normals, tick throws, Collarbone Breaker (6MP overhead)

6. Punish what they throw outHave one reliable combo for each common punish: a blocked reversal, an unsafe special, a whiffed heavy.See the Punish Finder

When to expect jumps

Be most ready to anti-air when the opponent is cornered (jumping is the most obvious way out) and when they're far behind on health (desperation makes players jump). Anti-air them there and they run out of options.

Why a plan beats reactions

Without a plan, you only win when the opponent makes a mistake. With one, every exchange has a purpose: you know what you want in neutral, after a knockdown and in the corner, and you notice sooner when something stops working. Change the tools to fit your character; keep the structure.

Risk and Reward

Most decisions in a fight are bets on what the opponent will do. Risk and reward is how you price those bets: compare what you gain if you're right with what you lose if you're wrong. There is almost never one correct option, only better and worse bets.

Example: wakeup uppercut or block?

You've been knocked down and you think they'll attack. With SF6 Ryu's numbers (10,000 health):

  • Uppercut, and you're right: HP Shoryuken deals 1,400 (14%) and knocks them down.
  • Uppercut, and they block or bait it: HP Shoryuken is −39 on block, so you eat their best punish, a Punish Counter combo that often deals 3,000 or more (30%+).
  • Block, and they throw you: 1,200 (12%).
  • Block, and they strike: you take nothing and stay in the round.

The uppercut risks more than twice what it can win, while blocking risks a throw. That doesn't mean "never uppercut"; it means uppercut when you've seen them attack on your wakeup, not out of habit. The more resources the opponent has (Drive Gauge for a Drive Rush combo, Super Art to end the punish), the bigger the risk.

Example: a fireball from mid-screen

At half-screen, a fireball deals a few hundred damage if it hits. If the opponent reads it and jumps, you eat a jump-in combo worth several times that. Against a player who jumps, it's a bad bet from that range; against one who blocks, it's a good one. On the other side, blocking fireballs in SF6 costs a little Drive Gauge, not health (except in Burnout), so walking forward through them is mostly a test of nerve.

Questions to ask before you commit

  • If it works, what do I get? Damage, a knockdown, the corner, Drive Gauge?
  • If it fails, what do I lose? Is it punishable on block or on whiff?
  • How many times have I seen them do the thing this beats?
  • What do they have right now to punish me with?
  • Do I even need this risk? When you're ahead on health or they're in the corner, usually not.

How Often Do You Need to Be Right?

Annie Duke's Thinking in Bets adds a useful step to the questions above: put a rough number on the bet. Take the wakeup uppercut example. If it works you gain about 1,400; if they block or bait it you lose about 3,000. Ignoring everything else (the knockdown, the corner, Drive Gauge), the uppercut only pays off if they attack on your wakeup more than about 2 times in 3: 1,400 × 2 = 2,800, still a bit short of one 3,000 punish. If you think they attack half the time, blocking is the better bet, even on the rounds where it doesn't work.

The second half of the lesson matters just as much: a good bet still loses. If you uppercut only when you're 80% sure, you'll still eat a punish 1 time in 5. When that happens, check the read, not the result. Did they really attack that often, or did you just want to press the button?

Matchup Analysis: The Prior Study

Before playing against a new character, you should study:

  • Optimal Zone: What range does this character control best?
  • Main Normals: What's their c.MK? Sweep? Poke?
  • Dangerous Specials: Which moves MUST you respect?
  • Weaknesses: What range are they weak in? What's vulnerable?
  • Meter Usage: How do they spend their Drive Gauge (Drive Rush, OD moves, Drive Impact) and their Super Art gauge? Do they hold a reversal super?
  • Throw Range: Exactly how far is their throw range?

Detailed Example: Study Zangief

Optimal Zone: Close range (throw range). Weak at long range.

Main Normals: 2MK (c.MK), a long low poke and his main footsies tool; 5LP (s.LP), +2 on block, which sets up his strike/throw mixup with Screw Piledriver; 6HP, +4 on block, for pressure. Anti-airs: 5HK (s.HK) and Double Lariat, whose air invincibility beats jumps but which are slower than a shoto's uppercut.

Dangerous Specials: Screw Piledriver (SPD: a command grab, so it can't be blocked or teched, with huge damage), OD Screw Piledriver (enhanced version), Double Lariat (projectile invincible, goes through fireballs), Level 3 Super Art (invincible command grab)

Weakness: No fireball, slow movement, and slow anti-airs. Projectiles force him to work to get in (though Drive Rush, Drive Impact and Double Lariat give him ways through).

Strategy: Maintain distance. Fireball spacing (don't throw them predictably at Lariat range). If he jumps, anti-air. Don't sit still in SPD range.

Pattern Recognition: Diagnosing Your Opponent (Deep Analysis)

Every player has patterns. Your job is to identify them EARLY and exploit them CONSISTENTLY. Pattern recognition is where casual players diverge from serious competitors.

The Observation Phase (Early Rounds)

Matches are usually best of 3 rounds, so you can't give rounds away. But alongside playing your solid baseline game, your main extra goal early is to gather data about how the opponent plays.

What to Watch For:

  • Poke Preference: Do they use c.MK or s.MK more? How far do they stand?
  • Spacing Habits: Do they maintain distance or rush in? Are they predictable about it?
  • Movement Patterns: Walk? Dash? Jump? How often? When?
  • Block Patterns: Do they crouch block? Stand block? Switch? Predictably?
  • Attack Patterns: Do they attack after blocking? Do they attack on wakeup always?
  • Meter Usage: Do they save or dump Drive Gauge and Super Art meter? Do they burn out often? Predictable about super timing?
Pattern Type How to Identify (Early) How to Exploit (Rounds 2-3) How They Counter (If They Adapt)
Jump Happy Round 1: They jump even when poked. Jump is their panic button. Rounds 2-3: Consistent anti-air every time they jump. Make them scared. They stop jumping. Now play ground footsies where you're stronger.
Attack Happy Round 1: Button mashing, no thought. Attacking constantly. Rounds 2-3: Whiff baits. Let them attack, whiff, you punish. Free damage. They become more patient. Now you must play MORE complex footsies.
Meter Dump Round 1: Gets meter, spends it immediately on super. Rounds 2-3: Stay patient and block. Most supers are very unsafe on block: block it, then punish hard (with your own super if you have one). They become meter-conscious. Now they save. But this makes them more predictable other ways.
Turtle Defense Round 1: Blocks EVERYTHING. Rarely presses buttons, rarely tries to interrupt. Rounds 2-3: Throw setups. Continuous poke conditioning → throw. Mix high/low/throw. They start pressing buttons to interrupt your pressure. Now you can bait (frame traps, shimmies) and punish attacks.
Predictable Throw Tech Round 1: Every time you walk in after a blockstring, they press throw tech (LP+LK). (In SF6 a single input techs both forward and back throws, so there is no "direction" to guess.) Rounds 2-3: Shimmy. Walk in, step back out of throw range, and punish the whiffed tech. Or use a frame trap: their tech attempt gets hit (often for a Punish Counter). They stop teching on habit. Now your real throws land. Back to the strike/throw mix.
Opener Habit Round 1: Their offense always opens with the same move (e.g., the same jump-in, or c.LP from the same range). Rounds 2-3: You're ready before it comes: anti-air the jump, beat the button with a faster or longer one, or Drive Parry it and punish. They vary their openers. More unpredictable. Forces you to play more reactively.
Wakeup Pattern Round 1: After knockdown, they do the same thing (block/attack/jump/throw tech). Rounds 2-3: Set up that specific counter every wakeup. Free damage/reset. They mix wakeup options. You must set up multiple layer mixup instead.

The Exploitation Phase (Rounds 2-3): Punishment Timing

Once you've identified a pattern, punish it IMMEDIATELY and CONSISTENTLY. Don't mix it up.

Why Consistency Matters: If they jump once and you anti-air, they might jump again (maybe was just test). If they jump 3 times and you anti-air all 3, they LEARN you anti-air jumps. Consistency embeds the lesson.

Counter-Adaptation Timeline:

  • (Rough heuristic, not a rule) After 1-2 exploitations: They notice. Might be accident.
  • After 3-5 exploitations: They KNOW you're countering this pattern. They'll start adapting.
  • After 5-10 exploitations: They've adapted. The pattern is dead. You must find the NEXT pattern.

False Patterns: The Observation Trap

Just because opponent did something twice doesn't mean it's a pattern. Example: opponent jumped twice = might be coincidence, not habitual.

Real Pattern Threshold: A good rule of thumb: 3+ repetitions in similar circumstances = pattern. After 3, it's reasonable to start exploiting.

Avoid Over-Reading: Many intermediate players see a pattern and exploit it once, succeed, then keep exploiting until opponent adapts and punishes them. They should have VARIED after the pattern died.

Dynamic Adaptation: The Meta Evolves Mid-Match

Your initial strategy might work Round 1, but opponent adapts Round 2. Your job is to adapt back. This creates layers of adaptation—a "mental chess" between both players.

Adaptation Options When Losing

You're losing to a specific tactic. You have several responses, each with trade-offs:

  • Option 1: Direct Counter → Do exactly what beats their pattern
    • Pros: If they repeat pattern, you win hard
    • Cons: If they adapt, you're stuck. Zero flexibility.
  • Option 2: Ignore It → Stop trying to beat this one thing
    • Pros: You can play YOUR game, not their game
    • Cons: You keep losing to the same thing
  • Option 3: Change Approach Entirely → Completely invert strategy
    • Pros: Unpredictable. Opponent doesn't know your gameplan.
    • Cons: You leave comfort zone. Execution becomes less consistent.
  • Option 4: Mix Options 1 & 2 → Sometimes counter, sometimes ignore
    • Pros: Keeps opponent guessing
    • Cons: Requires high skill. Easy to execute poorly.

Adaptation Timing: When to Change Strategy

Changing strategy too early = you give up on good gameplan. Changing too late = you lose entire round.

Strategic Timeline:

  • Round 1 (Observation): Play your baseline strategy. Don't adapt yet. Gather data.
  • Round 2 (Exploitation): You've identified patterns. Start exploiting. If it works, great. If not, prepare adjustment.
  • Round 3 (Major Change): If Round 2 exploitation failed, change your approach in a big way: different spacing, different poke patterns, different meter usage. Stay within tools you can execute reliably.

The Golden Rule of Adaptation

If you're winning: Don't change anything. Make the opponent be the one who has to figure it out. Keep doing it until they adapt.

If you're losing to the same thing repeatedly: Change. What you're doing is not working. Try something different (but see the Adaptation Trap below: one loss is not enough evidence).

Why This Works: Fighting games reward consistency within a gameplan. But when that plan fails, persistence = loss. Adaptation = survival.

Recognizing Adaptation From Your Opponent

As you exploit a pattern, opponent will eventually adapt. Recognizing THIS moment is crucial.

Signs They're Adapting:

  • Pattern stopped appearing (they noticed you're countering it)
  • They're doing something NEW you haven't seen
  • They're mixing up their approach (hard to read)
  • Your go-to punish is suddenly not working

When You See These Signs: STOP exploiting the old pattern. It's dead. Start observation again for NEW patterns. The meta has shifted.

The Adaptation Trap

Many players over-adapt. They see one loss to a tactic and immediately change strategy. This is wrong. Real adaptation requires:

  • Multiple losses to the same tactic (3+)
  • Confirmation they understand WHY it's working
  • Then, and only then, change strategy

Changing after one loss = you're playing scared, not strategically.

Yomi, Deception and Critical Moments

The second half of Playing to Win applies Sun Tzu's The Art of War to competitive games. These are the ideas that carry over most directly to SF6.

Yomi Layers: Why the Guessing Game Has an End

Yomi (読み, "reading") is knowing what the opponent will do. Sirlin's point is that a good game lets you counter anything you predict, and that the counters loop: you only need three layers, because the fourth brings you back to the start. The classic SF6 example, after a blocked string that leaves you plus and close:

  1. Your best option is a throw (layer 0).
  2. The opponent learns to tech it (layer 1).
  3. You shimmy: walk back, let their tech whiff and punish it (layer 2).
  4. They stop teching and simply block, which leaves nothing for the shimmy to punish (layer 3).
  5. And what beats someone who only blocks? The throw. You're back at the start.

You don't need to think "he knows that I know that he knows". You need to know the loop and figure out where this opponent is in it right now. Sirlin also points out a funny side effect: a beginner who always throws can catch an expert who was expecting the shimmy. The expert adjusts as soon as they realize who they're playing.

Hide Your Sweet Spot and Your Gaps

Every matchup has a sweet spot: a range where your moves work and theirs don't. Sirlin's example from Street Fighter II is Ryu standing just outside the reach of Ken's sweep, where the sweep whiffs and gets punished, fireballs are easy to block on reaction and jumps are easy to anti-air. Good players fight for that range and hide it, moving back and forth across it with safe moves, so the opponent keeps attacking from just out of range without understanding why.

The same goes for weaknesses. Almost every pressure sequence or fireball trap has gaps. A skilled player hides which gaps are real, leaves some open as bait and waits for the obvious "clever" reversal at the first opening. If your escape is the first moment anyone would think of, your opponent thought of it too.

Ahead or Behind: Different Rules

  • When you're winning, avoid critical moments. Don't create guessing games you don't need. With a big life lead, back off instead of risking a 50/50 on their wakeup; the opponent is the one who needs a big moment.
  • When you're losing, create them. You need situations where one correct guess changes the round.
  • With the opponent almost dead, your fastest jab finishes the round just as well as your slowest heavy, so use the safe option.
  • After taking big damage, don't act at the first possible moment. That's when everyone acts, and the opponent is waiting for it. Waiting a second or two buries the moment they were counting on.
  • Know when not to fight. If a disadvantage is temporary, play safe until it passes: in Burnout, stay out of the corner and wait for your gauge to come back. When the opponent is the one in Burnout, that's your moment to push.

Presence of Mind: Making Time Slow Down

Sirlin calls presence of mind the ability to see every moment of a fast situation, sometimes with the feeling that time has slowed down. It comes from knowing exactly where the decisions are. A string you don't know goes by in a blur. Once you know that the overhead can only come at two specific points, you stop watching everything and wait for a single cue, and the decision becomes easy. In SF6, learn where your opponent's strings and pressure can branch (after which button an overhead or a throw can come) and watch only for that.

The flip side: the best players know this too and try to take it away from you. Something strange and unexpected leaves you thinking "what was that?" for a moment, and that's exactly when they hit you with something you'd normally see coming.

Tricks Only Take You So Far

Sirlin describes "brick walls": simple tricks the opponent has to solve before the real fight even starts. He once won a tournament final with Rose by repeating a single low punch at a precise range, at one point eighteen times in a row. Brick walls win plenty of early-round matches. But the players who reach the top of a bracket get past them, even if they have to work out the answer mid-set. Above a certain level, you have to actively win instead of waiting for the opponent to hand you the match.

Pressure & Offense: Maintaining Initiative

There are 3 ways to apply pressure:

1) Hit Pressure (Blockstring)

Concept: Series of hits, some connect, some are blocked, but no gaps.

Example: c.LP → c.LP → c.MK, cancel into Drive Rush (costs 3 Drive bars) → s.MP and keep going. A normal done out of Drive Rush gets +4 extra frames of advantage on hit or block. (Avoid ending on a blocked sweep: sweeps are very unsafe on block.)

Goal: Opponent is blocking. They're passive. You control the pace.

2) Frame Advantage Pressure

Concept: You have +frames. You act first. Opponent must react.

Example: A move that is +2 on block, followed by a 4-frame jab. Their own 4-frame jab can't interrupt it; only an invincible reversal beats it outright (Drive Parry or Drive Reversal can still stop the pressure). Check your character's frame data: many light normals are minus on block.

Tool: Specials that are plus on block automatically generate pressure.

3) Mixup Pressure

Concept: You alternate between different options to create 50/50s.

Example: Walk up → throw, walk up → frame-trap strike, walk up → step back (shimmy) to bait their tech. They never know.

Psychology: Fear. Uncertainty. They make wrong choice.
