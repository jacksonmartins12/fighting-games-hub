Progression & Structured Learning

UniversalThe ideas in this chapter apply to any fighting game. Examples come from SF6, and sections marked SF6 only apply to it. For how other games differ, see Fighting Game Families.

The Optimal Learning Sequence: Structured Progression Over Months

Don't study everything at once. There's a logical progression based on skill layering. You build foundation, then add complexity. This section breaks down Month 1 to Month 7+ with specific milestones. These months are a study plan, not the four phases from the Introduction: many players hit the plateau (Phase 3) somewhere around months 4 to 6, which is exactly where this plan adds pattern recognition and matchup study. The same timeline is used in Training Regimens, the Visual Map and Resources.

Week 1-2: Absolute Fundamentals (The Triangle)

Focus: Attack, Block, Throw. ONLY these.

Time Allocation: 90% Play, 10% Learn

Training:

  • Play CPU Arcade Mode 2 hours/day
  • Don't memorize combos yet. Don't learn specials.
  • Only press attack, hold block, do throw (when close)
  • Understand: which beats which? When?

Success Metric: You win at least 1 round using only A-B-T against Lv3 CPU

Mental Milestone: "I understand Rock-Paper-Scissors applies here"

Week 3-4: Special Move Execution (Dexterity Training)

Focus: 2 special moves (Fireball + Dragon Punch typically)

Time Allocation: 70% Play, 30% Training Mode

Training:

  • 15 min training mode daily: Practice 1 special move
  • Start slow (1 input per second)
  • Gradually increase speed
  • Goal: 80%+ success rate at match speed

Match Play:

  • Play against CPU Lv2-3
  • Use these 2 specials freely. Your fundamentals will carry.

Success Metric: Both specials come out 8/10 times in match

Mental Milestone: "My hands can do the inputs. It's automatic now"

Week 5-8: Bread & Butter Combos (Damage Output)

Focus: 3 basic combos for your character

Time Allocation: 50% Play, 50% Training Mode

Training:

  • Combo 1: Starter into 2-3 hits (Easy, 10% damage)
  • Combo 2: Starter into special move (Medium, 20% damage)
  • Combo 3: Full combo with meter (Hard, 30%+ damage)
  • Drill each combo 50x until you can do it consistently

Match Play:

  • Play against CPU Lv3-4
  • Focus on LANDING the starter, then execute combo
  • Don't worry about fancy combos yet

Success Metric: You can land each combo 7/10 times in match

Mental Milestone: "When I hit opponent, damage follows automatically"

Week 9-12: Footsies Fundamentals (Mid-Range Control)

Focus: Footsies Elements 1-3 (Baiting, Whiff Punish, Throw Setup)

Time Allocation: 70% Play, 30% Deliberate Practice

Deliberate Practice:

  • Restricted sets: with a training partner (or a recorded training dummy), agree on no jumps and no specials. Only normals & throws.
  • Your goal: Control mid-range with pokes
  • Focus on spacing: how far can your poke reach?

Match Play Against Humans:

  • Play casual online matches against beginner-level players
  • You're learning against real players now (different from CPU)
  • Lose is OK. You're gathering data.

Success Metric: You win 40%+ of casual matches against similar level players

Mental Milestone: "Fighting games aren't just combos. It's spacing and reads"

Month 4: Intermediate Gameplan (Personal Strategy)

Focus: Advanced footsies (Elements 4-9), Pattern recognition, Basic adaptation

Time Allocation: 80% Play, 20% Analysis

Key Activities:

  • Play online ranked / small online tournaments
  • After each match, analyze (using framework from Psychology section)
  • Identify opponent patterns. Exploit 1-2 per match.
  • Start experimenting with different approaches

Skill Expansion:

  • Learn 5-7 combos (situational, not just BnB)
  • Learn basic okizeme (wakeup setups)
  • Learn character-specific matchup tips

Success Metric: You beat intermediate players (50%+ win rate) and can explain why you won each match

Mental Milestone: "I'm not just reacting. I'm planning and reading"

Month 5-6: Advanced Gameplan (Depth & Consistency)

Focus: Deep footsies (all 35 elements), Tournament-level execution, Matchup mastery

Time Allocation: 60% Play, 40% Targeted Practice

Targeted Practice:

  • Spend 2 hours labbing ONE matchup (character you struggle with)
  • Identify your gameplan against THAT character
  • Test it in 10 casual matches
  • Refine based on results

Match Play:

  • Play ranked actively (50+ matches/week)
  • Aim for 55%+ win rate
  • Identify top 3 players who beat you. Study their play.

Mental Preparation:

  • Join community. Play locals weekly.
  • Prepare for first tournament
  • Record matches. Review your own play like you're analyzing opponent

Success Metric: You hold a stable or rising rank and can explain every loss with a specific reason

Mental Milestone: "There's no skill ceiling. I'll keep improving indefinitely"

Month 7+: Refinement & Specialization (Infinite Depth)

Focus: Niche tech, deep matchup knowledge, psychological depth, unique playstyle

Activities:

  • Find your character's frame data quirks. Learn tech others don't know about.
  • Study top players' play. What makes them different?
  • Develop PERSONAL gameplan (not copy others)
  • Compete in tournaments. Travel if serious.
  • Start teaching beginners. Solidify your own knowledge.

Improvement Never Stops: Even pros keep refining their play year after year. Fighting games have enormous depth.

Mental Milestone: "I'm a competitor. This is my identity now"

Character Selection & Commitment

Core Principle: Your First Main Doesn't Have to Be Your Last

Stop overthinking character selection. The "perfect" character for you doesn't exist — you become perfect at the game by mastering one character deeply. Switching constantly is one of the most common beginner mistakes.

Why Commit to One Character?

Each character has 30-40+ unique moves. Each move has startup, active, recovery. Each situation (corner, open, airborne opponent) requires specific tools. Mastering a character takes months.

When you switch characters: You lose the character-specific knowledge (moves, combos, setups) and it feels like starting over. You're not really back to zero, though: fundamentals like blocking, anti-airing, spacing and reading opponents carry over, and each new character is easier to learn than the last. But constant switching means you never get deep with anyone.

Result: By month 6, committed players tend to be noticeably further along than constant switchers.

  • Pick based on FEELING, not Tier List: Does this character look cool? Do their moves feel satisfying? Do you want to practice them?
  • Commit for at least 3 months: Give yourself enough time to understand the character's identity.
  • After 3 months, reassess: Do you STILL love this character? If yes, continue. If no, switch once and commit again.
  • Allow yourself to explore in casual: Play other characters casually for fun. But have ONE main for serious practice/ranked.

The Secondary Character Trap

Many players want a secondary character "just in case." This splits focus. In reality, until you've moved past the intermediate stage (roughly your first six months), you don't have enough time/mental bandwidth for two characters. Master one first.

Learning Other Characters Without Switching Mains

There's another side to this. gootecks, who stuck to one or two characters for years, argues that one character isn't enough anymore, and he has a point. What he recommends isn't splitting your practice between two mains; it's learning the basics of many characters:

  • You understand both sides of the matchup. A short session with a character in training mode shows you what they fear, what's unsafe and what they're trying to do.
  • You learn more from watching. When you know a character's options, you can see where a player could have chosen differently.
  • You can trade knowledge with more players. Tips about their character often apply to yours.
  • Later, a pocket character can cover a bad matchup in tournaments. Infiltration used 14 characters in the 2015 season and won key sets with picks nobody expected.

The practical balance: keep one main for ranked and serious practice (SF6 tracks rank per character anyway), and spend some lab time with the characters you meet most, using the character checklist in Lab Exercises. Starting with a well-rounded character also helps. gootecks recommends learning the game through Ryu, because a shoto's plan (fireballs, pokes, anti-airs) teaches the fundamentals every other character uses.

Tournament Preparation & Competition

Readiness Checklist: Are You Ready to Play Competitively?

Before your first tournament (or ranked matches), check how many of these you can do consistently. Treat it as a to-do list, not an entry requirement:

Technical Readiness

  • Execute Your BnB Combos: Can you land your basic combo 9/10 times against moving CPU (Level 3+)?
  • Manage Resources: Do you consciously track your Drive Gauge, Super Art gauge and health? Not just blind offense?
  • Understand Matchup: Can you explain in 2-3 sentences what your character does well and poorly vs. 5 common matchups?
  • Anti-Air Consistently: When opponent jumps, does your anti-air come out reflexively?

Mental Readiness

  • Accept Losses: Can you lose and not blame lag/character/opponent? Or immediately rage?
  • Analyze Calmly: Can you watch a replay without tilting?
  • Relax Under Pressure: Do you get nervous? (Normal. Can you control it?)
  • Play for Learning: Tournament is first and foremost a chance to see high-level play and level up. Results are secondary.

Not Ready Yet?

If you're not checking most of these boxes, go anyway — just set your expectations accordingly. There is no skill level you need to reach before entering a tournament. Patrick Miller's book is explicit about this: everyone should enter, even with no shot at winning, and going 0-2 in your first several brackets is normal and nothing to be ashamed of.

Why? Tournament pressure can't be replicated in ranked or casuals. Use this checklist as a list of things to work on, not as a gate. Treat early tournaments as practice under pressure and bring your notes home.

Understanding Tournaments

Sirlin's first tournaments went worse than he expected, and his friend John Choi, who won that day, told him he simply wasn't used to tournaments yet. Sirlin disagreed at the time and later admitted Choi was right. A tournament is its own skill, with customs that can throw off excellent players:

  • Format: usually double elimination, with a losers bracket that gives you a second chance. Sets are often first to two, sometimes first to three near the end. Know it before you sit down.
  • Seeding puts the strongest players against the weakest early so they don't knock each other out. It isn't personal; don't spend energy on it.
  • Character changes: at most SF6 events, the loser of a game can switch characters while the winner keeps theirs. Decide beforehand whether you have a counterpick.
  • The flow of a set: over several games, one player often starts to figure the other out. Learn to notice when it's happening to you, and change something before the set is over.

Casual matches rarely measure much. In a tournament, even familiar opponents play better and more carefully, and they save their best tactics for it. That's why Sirlin insists that formal competition is the real test.

Preparing Without Cramming

  • Last-minute learning doesn't hold up under pressure. If you've been practicing consistently, you're already prepared.
  • Use the date to refocus. Isolate the techniques you usually get away with not doing and drill them, however boring.
  • Play your best character. Sirlin's comparison: in the same time, you could take a side character from 20 to 75 out of 100, or your main from 93 to 93.5, and your main is the one who will win you the tournament.
  • Know the meta: which characters and strategies are popular right now and, at higher levels, the habits of the few players who can actually beat you.
  • Prefer the character you're comfortable with over a tier list. Tier lists assume evenly matched players at full capacity, which is not what most tournament matches look like.

Staying Fresh on Tournament Day

Seth Killian's chapter on mental toughness in the book lists the traps that beat players who are good enough to win:

  • Fatigue: tournaments are long and draining, and many players play worse in the later rounds. Eat, drink water and rest between matches.
  • A simple plan for early rounds: a straightforward gameplan that weaker opponents have to solve saves energy and keeps your best stuff hidden.
  • Getting stuck in the past: a missed punish isn't a reason to throw away the rest of the set, and a lucky win isn't something to feel guilty about.
  • Worrying about the future: stop studying the bracket. Focus on the current match and, at most, the next opponent.
  • The urge to prove a point: throwing another fireball right after one got jumped, just to show you can, is fatigue talking.

Premortem and Backcasting: Plan From the End

Two exercises from Thinking in Bets work well in the week before an event. In backcasting, you imagine you reached your goal (out of pools, top 8 at your local, a target rank by the end of the season) and work backward: what had to go right? In a premortem, you imagine you went 0-2 and ask why. Duke prefers doing both, because imagining success alone tends to make plans too optimistic, while the premortem brings out the problems people don't like to mention.

"I went 0-2. Why?"What to do now

Lost to a matchup I almost never see onlineLook at who's registered and spend a session on the two characters you know least

After the event, watch out for hindsight bias, which Duke compares to a chainsaw: once one branch of the future happens, the others get cut off and the result looks like it was obvious all along. "I should have known he'd reversal" is usually wrong; ask instead what you knew at the time.

What Makes the Best Player?

Sirlin spent years asking what separates the number one player not from average players but from the rest of the top ten. He admits his conclusions are anecdotal, but they're worth knowing:

  • Tournament experience and deep knowledge of the game: everyone in the top ten has them. They get you in; they don't make you number one. In no game he looked at was the best player the one with the deepest knowledge.
  • Love of the game: it probably helps the best stay at the top longer, but plenty of weak players love their games too.
  • Mental toughness: necessary for long tournament days. Even Sirlin admits having thought it would be easier to lose the next round than to face the lineup ahead.
  • Attitude toward winning, losing and improving: players who always had a bad attitude rarely break through. Several number ones only became arrogant after reaching the top.
  • Technical skill: it never hurts, but the best players weren't the ones with the best execution.
  • Adaptability: many players named it among the three most important traits without being asked about it.
  • Yomi: the one trait that clearly belongs to the very best. Nobody fears the reads of a player who always finishes eighth.
  • Appraisal: judging the real value of moves, characters and resources. Sirlin ended up ranking it above yomi, because almost every decision tests it, while yomi only matters at certain moments.

Adaptability: Choi Against the Valle CC

At the B3 tournament, John Choi and Alex Valle met in the Street Fighter Alpha 2 final. Valle unveiled a technique nobody had seen, which would become the most powerful tactic in the game and was named the "Valle CC" after him. Choi didn't fully understand what was happening, but he realized the rules had changed. He adjusted during the set, got caught less and less, started using the technique back and lost only on the last hit of the last game. Sirlin calls it the greatest display of adaptability in fighting game history.

Sirlin contrasts the adaptive player with the planner, who studies the system until they "know the algorithm" for each situation. Sirlin himself was a planner, yet he found few planners among the number ones. The adaptive player doesn't need to know all five possible answers to a situation; they just know you're going to pick the fifth, and they're not falling for it.

Appraisal: Seeing the Game's Real Values

Most players share the conventional wisdom about what's good. The best see different values: they drop popular tactics that don't work against strong opponents and get a lot out of moves others think are useless. Often they can't fully explain why, which is why Sirlin advises watching what masters do rather than asking them why they do it. In SF6 this shows up in questions like how much a bar of Drive is really worth in a given situation, which buttons top players actually use in neutral, or which characters the tier lists underrate.

Sirlin separates two kinds of appraisal: understanding the system in general (what matters most in the game) and judging the specific situation in front of you (in this round, with these resources, this exchange is worth taking). Planners lean toward the first, adaptive players toward the second.

Community & Long-Term Growth

Fighting Games Are a Social Activity

The fastest way to improve is not more single-player practice. It's finding people at similar skill levels and playing together regularly. A stable group can speed up your improvement considerably.

Why Community Matters

  • Practice Against Real Players: CPU doesn't adapt. Real players find your patterns and punish them. You learn faster.
  • Different Perspectives: When you lose, your opponent can often tell you why and what they were looking for. CPU just beats you.
  • Consistency: Playing weekly with the same group means facing the same few opponents again and again, so you can study their habits (and they study yours). Random online matchmaking gives you a new opponent almost every match = more variety, but less pattern learning.
  • Emotional Support: Learning fighting games is frustrating. Doing it alone is worse. Others going through same struggle makes it bearable.
  • Shared Growth: Your group levels up together. In 6 months you've all grown. Bonds form.
  • Find Local Community: Discord, Reddit local communities, local arcades, fighting game venues
  • Start with Beginners: Find others at similar skill level. Don't jump straight to advanced players.
  • Create Repeating Meetups: Weekly or bi-weekly. Consistency matters more than frequency.
  • Share Knowledge: Teaching others solidifies your own knowledge. By month 6, teach newcomers what you've learned.
  • Respect the Grind: Everyone is on their own timeline. Don't compare progress. Just show up and play.

Teaching as Advanced Skill

Once you reach intermediate level, teaching becomes your next frontier. Teaching forces you to articulate why moves work and why strategies fail, which deepens your own understanding.

This is one reason many strong players coach or make guides: explaining something clearly exposes the gaps in your own understanding.

Who to Play Against

Sirlin's advice on choosing opponents as you learn:

  • Stronger players teach you what not to do. Every unsafe move gets punished. Watch exactly how they punish you and how they close out rounds.
  • But against them you rarely get to practice winning. Weaker players give you many chances to practice your offense and finishing rounds until it's automatic, so that when an expert finally slips, you capitalize without having to think.
  • Don't stay there too long. Weaker players don't punish your mistakes, fall for tricks that don't work on good players and often beat themselves, which can teach you that waiting is enough. When you're ready, focus on stronger opponents.
  • Get into the community early. That's where the knowledge about the game and the best opponents are.

Your Review Pod: Training Partners Who Tell You the Truth

Annie Duke credits much of her poker career to a small group of players who reviewed hands together under clear rules: the goal was getting closer to the truth, not making each other feel better. Most people's friends do the opposite and nod along with the bad-luck story. In fighting games, a review pod can be two or three training partners who watch each other's replays, or a channel in your local's Discord. What matters is the agreement behind it.

What the Group Agrees On

  • Accuracy over comfort. The group praises you for spotting your own mistake, not for a good excuse.
  • Accountability. You know you'll have to explain your decisions to them, so you think about them more carefully while you play. Duke says knowing she'd have to justify playing past her loss limit was often enough to stop her.
  • Different points of view. Players with different mains, play styles and ranks notice different things. A group where everyone plays the same way just confirms itself.

Four Rules for Reviewing Together

Duke borrows four norms that sociologist Robert Merton described for how science works, known as CUDOS. Adapted to replay review:

  • Share everything. Send the whole set, including the embarrassing rounds, not just the clip that supports your version of what happened.
  • Judge the idea, not the person. A lower-ranked friend can spot a habit you can't see; a Master player can be wrong about your matchup. Whenever Duke caught herself writing someone off as a bad player, she made herself find something they did well.
  • Hide the outcome. Show the clip up to the decision you're asking about and stop. Don't say how it ended or what you think you should have done. Duke's poker group reviewed hands this way, because knowing the result skews everyone's judgment.
  • Doubt politely. Ask "how sure are you?" and "what else could explain this?", especially about the conclusions everyone likes.

Venting or Reviewing?

Sometimes a friend just needs to complain about a bad loss, and that's fine. Duke suggests making it explicit. Ask first: do you want to vent or do you want to look at what happened? If it's review, it happens under the group's rules. If it's venting, everyone knows it isn't analysis.

The Art of Learning: Depth, Loss and Your Own Style

The other half of Josh Waitzkin's The Art of Learning is about how he learned two difficult games from scratch, chess as a child and Tai Chi Push Hands as an adult, and reached the top in both. His methods fit a fighting game well, because both games reward the same thing: deep, automatic understanding of a few situations rather than a long list of memorized tricks.

Talent or Skill You Build?

Waitzkin draws on psychologist Carol Dweck's research about two ways of seeing ability. Some people treat it as something fixed: you're good at a thing or you aren't. Others see it as something built step by step. When things get hard, the first group tends to give up or avoid the challenge, because failing seems to prove they lack talent; the second group treats difficulty as part of the process. Waitzkin saw it in young chess players: kids praised for being "naturals" often fell apart after their first big loss. The fighting game version is easy to hear: "I'm not a reactions person", "my hands are bad", "I'm just not a Master player". Compare it with "I can't anti-air jumps from far away yet". Only the second sentence gives you something to train.

Investment in Loss

When Waitzkin started Push Hands, the experienced students threw him around for months. Instead of stiffening up and using strength to avoid losing, he let himself get thrown and paid attention to how it happened. Meanwhile, students who protected their egos by resisting with muscle stopped improving. He called it investment in loss: accepting losses now in exchange for learning something that wins later.

For SF6 players, it means:

  • Play casual sets against people who beat you, and keep playing after the first few losses.
  • Use the new option you're learning even when it costs you the round. Losing while trying the right thing is progress; winning with your old crutch isn't.
  • Look for the matchup you dread instead of avoiding it.
  • Keep this separate from the matches you're trying to win (see The Practice Mindset).

Waitzkin also points out that it's harder to stay a beginner once people expect things from you. A player with a high rank has the most to lose from looking bad, and that's exactly the moment it pays to keep investing in loss.

Making Smaller Circles

Waitzkin's chess teacher started him with simplified positions, like a few pawns and the two kings, while many kids his age memorized openings. Openings won games early, but the kids who learned principles in simple positions understood the game better later on. In Tai Chi he did the same: he would take one movement and repeat it thousands of times, starting large and exaggerated and making it smaller and more efficient, until a tiny motion carried the same power. He calls it making smaller circles: going deep into a small piece of the game until you understand the principles behind it.

The SF6 version: instead of learning ten oki setups, take one situation, like what you do after your Drive Rush gets blocked, and study it to the bottom: every option you have, what each one beats, and what the opponent can do about it. Or take one tool, like your best poke, and learn every range it works at. A few situations understood deeply will carry over to many others. It's the same idea behind the one-weakness-at-a-time approach in the Lab Exercises.

Numbers to Leave Numbers

Chess beginners learn that a knight is worth three pawns and a rook five. Masters know those numbers so well that they stop thinking about them and see the position instead. Waitzkin calls this learning the numbers to leave the numbers. Research on chess players backs up how it works: strong players remember real game positions far better than weak ones, but no better when the pieces are placed at random, because what they remember are familiar patterns, groups of pieces stored as one unit.

Frame data works the same way. At first you check that a move is −6 and work out what punishes it. Later you just know the move is unsafe and your hands punish it. The numbers were a stage, not the goal. Waitzkin also describes how this makes time feel slower: with enough patterns stored, the expert sees the same half-second in much more detail. That's why a Drive Impact you used to eat every time starts to look slow once you've seen it enough.

Your Style Is Yours

As a kid, Waitzkin's best chess came in wild, complicated positions, which matched his personality. Later he worked with two very different coaches: one known for breaking students down and rebuilding them his own way, and one who built on what each student already did well. Waitzkin's conclusion is that you should absorb new knowledge without losing what makes you play well; his mother, who trained horses, had seen the same difference between breaking a horse by force and guiding it.

In SF6, tier lists, coaches and pro players are full of good advice. Take it, but notice when it's pulling you away from the way you naturally play. If you love scrambles and pressure, forcing yourself into a patient zoning character because it's ranked higher may make you worse, not better. Fix your gaps, but build on your strengths.

Try This This Week

  • Write down one "I'm just bad at..." sentence you use and rewrite it as a skill you haven't built yet.
  • Choose one small situation and study it to the bottom before moving on to the next.
  • Play one casual set where your only goal is using the option you're learning, whatever the score.
  • Note one thing you do well that no tier list or coach taught you. Keep it.

The Path of Mastery: Learning to Love the Plateau

George Leonard, who started aikido at 47 and went on to found his own dojo, wrote Mastery (1991) about learning any skill over the long term. His starting point: progress doesn't follow a straight line. It comes in short spurts, each followed by a slight drop and then a long plateau, usually a little higher than the last one. Most of your time as a player will be spent on plateaus, so how you handle them decides how far you go. (The tracker has practical tips for when your numbers stop moving.)

The Plateau Isn't Wasted Time

Leonard's explanation: when you learn something new, you have to think about every part of it, and thinking gets in the way of fluid movement. With enough repetition the skill moves to "autopilot", and only then does it look like a sudden jump. The learning was happening all along, on the plateau.

Two consequences for SF6. First, every new layer makes things fall apart for a while: when you start adding Drive Rush to your neutral or hit-confirming instead of throwing out a raw combo, you'll play worse, because you're thinking again. Second, you can't skip stages: each one depends on the one before, and advanced pressure doesn't help much while your basic combos still drop in matches.

Three Ways to Get Stuck

  • The Dabbler: loves beginnings: a new character, a new game, fast early progress. When the first plateau arrives, they switch characters or games and start over, telling themselves "this one doesn't suit me". Years later, they've played everything and mastered nothing (see the Secondary Character Trap above).
  • The Obsessive: wants results now. When progress slows, they double the hours, grind ranked all night and drill until their hands hurt. The result is a spike, a crash, burnout or a wrist injury.
  • The Hacker: once they're comfortable, they stay there. Leonard compares them to the tennis player with a solid forehand who decides he can live with a ragged backhand; the SF6 version is solid offense with a defense that falls apart on wakeup. They play the same matches with the same habits for years and never work on what's missing.

When Leonard asked audiences which of the three described them, the answers split into roughly even thirds. You can also be different types in different areas: dedicated in the lab and a Dabbler with characters, for example.

The Master's Way: Practice for Its Own Sake

The alternative is to keep practicing on the plateau, and to enjoy it, not only the spurts. Leonard describes the moment this clicked for him in aikido: after years of being frustrated by each new plateau, he caught himself thinking, "Oh boy. Another plateau. Good. I can just stay on it and keep practicing. Sooner or later, there'll be another spurt."

That doesn't mean training lightly. Unlike the Hacker, the master works hard on the plateau; what changes is that the next rank stops being the only reason to play. Leonard tells how, soon after he and three other brown belts started talking about making black belt, all four got seriously hurt within three weeks. They only got there after going back to steady practice without staring at the goal. The paradox: the people who stop chasing the next spurt are the ones who keep getting them.

Leonard's Five Keys, Applied to SF6

  1. Instruction: a coach, a stronger friend or a good guide speeds everything up; self-taught players often spend years reinventing the wheel. To judge a teacher, look at their students, not just their rank, because a great player isn't necessarily a good teacher. Good teachers point out what you're doing right about as often as what you're doing wrong (Leonard cites basketball coach John Wooden, who kept roughly a fifty-fifty balance) and have patience with beginners. Videos and guides help, but they can't watch you play, so they can't tell you what you're getting wrong. And if a teacher isn't working for you, it's fine to move on.
  2. Practice: Leonard treats practice as a path, not a chore. Masters enjoy the basics: where a beginner gets bored after ten repetitions, an experienced player notices the small differences between one anti-air and the next. An old martial arts saying puts it simply: the master is the one who stays on the mat five minutes longer every day than anybody else.
  3. Surrender: be willing to look clumsy as a beginner, and to give up part of what you already do well to reach the next level. Leonard calls it letting go of the cup to grab the quart: fixing a habit often means playing worse for a while. It's the hardest key for players coming from other fighting games. Leonard tells of two karate black belts in his aikido class: one kept falling back on his old style and fell behind complete beginners; the other trained like a beginner and progressed fast. For a master, there are no experts, only learners.
  4. Intentionality: what goes on in your head matters. In one study Leonard cites, karate beginners who combined relaxation with mental rehearsal of their techniques did best in sparring. Picture the situation you're training, watch top players, and do each rep with full attention instead of on autopilot.
  5. The edge: masters are obsessed with fundamentals and, at the same time, push their limits. A tournament can play the role of a belt exam: an intense goal that exposes every weakness. But the day after the exam, the new black belt is back on the mat. Push your edge, then go back to the path.

Why You Slide Back Into Old Habits

Leonard borrows the idea of homeostasis: every system, including your body and your habits, resists change, even good change. When you change something big (switching from Modern to Classic, adopting a more patient style), you'll play worse for a while, and everything in you will push to go back. That doesn't mean the change is wrong; it means it's really changing something. The resistance can also come from the people around you: your usual sparring partners may not like it when you stop playing the way they're used to. His guidelines:

  • Expect resistance. The urge to go back will come; take it as a sign that something is changing, not as a reason to panic.
  • Negotiate. Don't back off and don't bull your way through; be willing to take one step back for every two forward. Change one thing at a time. Change your controls, your main and your play style all at once, and you'll go back on all of them.
  • Build a support system: friends, a Discord, a training partner who knows what you're working on.
  • Practice regularly: a regular practice gives you a stable base while everything else is changing.
  • Keep learning: treat SF6 as something you'll learn for years, not a finish line.

Finding Energy to Practice

Leonard argues that we gain energy by using it, and lists ways to find more. The ones that fit fighting games best:

  • Set priorities: you can't work on everything at once. Pick what matters this week and accept leaving the rest for later. As far as energy goes, a wrong choice is better than no choice.
  • Make commitments: at his dojo, students who signed up for a belt exam as soon as the list went up trained with visibly more energy than those who signed up at the last minute. Signing up for a local or online tournament weeks ahead, and telling people about it, works the same way.
  • Acknowledge the negative: admitting what's going wrong (talking about it with a friend, writing it down) frees energy; pretending everything is fine drains it.
  • Put frustration to work: anger carries a lot of energy. Instead of pouring it into another ranked match while tilted, put it into a lab session on exactly what beat you.
  • Stay physically fit: it adds energy to everything else you do.

Pitfalls Along the Way

  • Obsessing over the goal: if reaching Master is all that matters, every match is an exam and every loss is a failure. Leonard's image: know the peak is up ahead, but keep your eyes on the path.
  • Too little competitiveness: not playing all out to win cheapens the game and disrespects your opponent. Win graciously and lose with the same grace.
  • Too much competitiveness: if winning is the only thing, then practice and fundamentals are nothing, and every loss feels like a disaster.
  • Inconsistency: a five-hour session followed by a week off teaches less than 45 minutes a day. A fixed pre-session ritual (the same warm-up in the same order) helps you get into focus, the way some surgeons scrub in exactly the same way before every operation. Missing a few sessions isn't a reason to quit.
  • Perfectionism: watching only top players' best moments makes everything you do look bad. Mastery isn't perfection; it's trying, failing and trying again, for years.
  • Vanity: not wanting to look bad, so you never try anything new where people can see.
  • Dead seriousness: losing the ability to laugh at yourself. A sense of humor makes the plateau bearable.
  • Prizes: in studies Leonard cites, children rewarded with gold stars learned faster at first, then leveled off, and when the stars stopped they fell below children who never got any. If you only play for the rank badge, motivation disappears when the badge stops moving.
  • Injury: Leonard argues that most serious injuries come from getting ahead of yourself; avoiding them is less about being cautious than about being aware. Hand and wrist pain from long sessions is real. Take breaks and stop when it hurts.

The Practice Journal: Deliberate Self-Reflection

Your Most Powerful Learning Tool: A Simple Notebook

Most fighting game players practice without reflecting. They grind combos, play matches, then move on. Patrick Miller makes note-taking a core habit from the very first session in his book: jotting down questions, what you practiced and what to explore next is how you reflect on the game separately from playing it. It's the difference between spinning your wheels and actual improvement.

What to Track

  • Session Date & Duration: When did you practice? How long?
  • Today's Focus: What specific skill are you working on? (Example: "Anti-air reactions" not "improve")
  • Practice Breakdown: 20 min training mode, 30 min vs CPU, 20 min casual matches
  • Wins & Losses: In casual, did you win? Against whom? Why?
  • One Thing That Worked: What did you do well today? Replicate this.
  • One Thing That Failed: What is your weakness? This is your next focus.
  • Questions for Next Time: "How do I beat this mixup?" "Why does that move beat mine?"
  • Emotional State: Did you stay calm? Get tilted? This matters for your growth.
Example Practice Journal Entry: DATE: Month 3, Week 2, Wednesday DURATION: 1.5 hours FOCUS: Anti-air consistency against crossups BREAKDOWN: - 15 min warm-up (basic combos, movement) - 25 min training mode: Practice anti-air DP against jump-in patterns - 30 min vs CPU Level 5: Play full matches, practice anti-air mid-game - 20 min casual online: Fight 3 players RESULTS: Won 2/3 casual matches. Lost to strong crossup player. WHAT WORKED: - Anticipated jump and anti-aired early 2x successfully - Stayed calm even when opponent mixed me up WHAT FAILED: - Still get hit by crossups when I try to DP (unsure of timing/side) - Sometimes panic and throw = opponent techs free - Meter management still sloppy (spent all Super Art gauge in round 1, had nothing in round 3) NEXT FOCUS: - Deep dive on crossup anti-air timing (watch CPU replay frame-by-frame?) - Practice throw confirms (when to throw vs attack) - Meter allocation strategy EMOTIONAL: Slightly frustrated but productive. Taking a 5-min break when tilted helped. QUESTION: What's the difference between crossup DP timing vs normal DP timing?

Why This Works

Externalize Knowledge: Your brain has limited working memory. Writing things down frees mental space for new learning.

Identify Patterns: After 2 weeks of notes, you see patterns: "Oh, I always lose to this mixup" or "I'm getting better at anti-air". Without notes, it feels random.

Motivational: Seeing "improved anti-air from 40% to 70%" is concrete progress. Feels good. Keeps you going.

Prevents Wasted Practice: Without notes, you might practice the wrong thing. Notes force you to think: "What should I practice today?"

Don't Over-Complicate

The note-taking doesn't need to be perfect. A phone note, a text file, a physical notebook. Consistency matters more than format. Spend 5 minutes post-session jotting things down. That's it.
