Ranked is where most players spend their online time, and it's also where most frustration comes from. Part of that frustration is simply not knowing how the system works: why a loss cost so much, why you didn't drop after a bad streak, why your second character starts from zero. This chapter explains the rules as they stand in late 2026 (Season 4), then turns to the part that actually matters: what kind of opponents you'll meet as you climb, and how to use ranked as practice instead of letting it use you.

If you only remember one thing from this chapter: your rank is a slow, noisy measurement of your results, not a grade of your skill. Climb by improving specific skills, check them in the Progress Tracker, and let the League Points follow.

A note on accuracy. Capcom has changed parts of ranked several times since launch, mostly at the top of the ladder. The numbers below are the ones reported consistently by Capcom's announcements and community coverage. Capcom doesn't publish every detail (for example, exact LP per match), so where something isn't public, this chapter says so instead of guessing. If the game's own rank screen ever disagrees with this page, trust the game.

The Basics: Leagues, Ranks and Placement

SF6 has eight ranks, from lowest to highest: Rookie, Iron, Bronze, Silver, Gold, Platinum, Diamond and Master. Every rank from Rookie to Diamond is split into five divisions, numbered 1 to 5, where 5 is the highest. Gold 5 is one step away from Platinum 1. Master has no numbered divisions; it uses a separate rating instead, explained below.

Your rank belongs to your character, not your account

Unlike Street Fighter V, SF6 tracks rank separately for each character. A Master Ken player who picks up Manon starts Manon's ranked journey from scratch, and losing twenty games in a row with Manon doesn't touch Ken's rank. This is good news: you can learn a second character in ranked without risking your main's rank.

Placement matches

The first time you play ranked with a character, the game asks you to pick an experience level from four options: Beginner, Novice, Intermediate or Advanced. You then play placement matches (ten of them) and the game sets your starting rank for that character from your results. Picking Beginner keeps you at the bottom; the higher options let you place higher.

Be honest when you choose. Placing too high doesn't make you better; it just means a long losing streak while the system corrects itself. Placing too low costs you a few easy wins and some time. Of the two, placing low is the cheaper mistake.

Does Modern or Classic matter?

Both Classic and Modern controls are allowed in ranked; Dynamic controls are offline only. There is no separate Modern ladder: you play against both control types, and the rank is attached to the character. Modern trades some options and damage for simpler inputs, which is a fair trade at every level of the ladder. Pick the scheme you'd want to play long term, because what you build in training is tied to how you play the character.

League Points and Rank Protection

From Rookie to Diamond, your rank is decided by League Points (LP). You gain LP for wins and, from Iron upward, lose LP for losses. When your total crosses a threshold, you move up a division. These are the thresholds as they have been reported since launch:

Rank LP to reach division 1 LP per division
Rookie 0 200
Iron 1,000 400
Bronze 3,000 400
Silver 5,000 800
Gold 9,000 800
Platinum 13,000 1,200
Diamond 19,000 1,200
Master 25,000 No divisions (uses Master Rate)

Notice how the steps get bigger. Going from Rookie to Silver takes 5,000 LP; going from Platinum 1 to Master takes 12,000. The climb feels fast at the start and slow near the top, and that's by design, not a sign that you stopped improving.

How much LP a match is worth

Capcom doesn't publish an exact formula, so don't trust anyone who quotes a fixed number per win. What is known:

  • Your opponent's rank matters. Beating a higher-ranked player earns more; losing to a lower-ranked player costs more than losing to someone at your level.
  • Win streaks give bonus LP from Rookie to Gold. This exists to push improving players toward their real level quickly. From Platinum upward, there is no streak bonus.
  • In Rookie, losses cost no LP. It is a safe space to learn the game online.

Demotion and protection

The game's rank rules describe four groups:

Ranks Win streak bonus What protects you
Rookie Yes No LP lost on defeat
Iron to Gold Yes No demotion out of this group (you can't fall back to Rookie), plus a one-time rank-down protection
Platinum and Diamond No One-time rank-down protection only; a losing streak can send you back down (for example, from Platinum to Gold)
Master No Master is a floor: once a character reaches it, it never drops back to Diamond

The one-time protection works as a cushion right after you rank up: the game gives you one save before a loss can drop you back. Capcom hasn't documented every edge case, so the practical summary is this: the lower ranks are forgiving, Platinum and Diamond are where a bad streak can actually cost you a rank, and Master is permanent.

Example: you hit Platinum 1 after a good evening, then lose four in a row the next day. The first loss that would have sent you back to Gold is absorbed by the protection. If the slide continues, you can drop to Gold 5. That's not a disaster: Gold 5 is 800 LP from where you were, and the win streak bonus in Gold helps you get back. The real damage would be playing twenty more angry games to "fix it".

Master and Master Rate

When a character reaches 25,000 LP, it enters Master and the climb changes completely. Master players are ranked by Master Rate (MR), a separate rating that only moves in matches between two Master players. Everyone starts in Master at 1500 MR.

How MR works

MR behaves like a classic rating system: how much you gain or lose depends on the gap between your MR and your opponent's. Beating someone with much higher MR earns a lot; losing to someone with much lower MR costs a lot; even matches move you a moderate amount. There is no streak bonus and no protection, but there's also no way to drop out of Master. MR can fall below 1500, and that's normal: 1500 is the starting point, not a minimum.

The Master tiers

Since the February 2025 update, Master is split into titles based on MR:

Title Requirement
Master Reach 25,000 LP (starts at 1500 MR)
High Master 1600 MR
Grand Master 1700 MR
Ultimate Master 1800 MR
Legend Not a fixed MR value: it goes to the top 500 players on the Master Rate leaderboard

Legend is a moving target. To keep it, you have to stay inside the top 500 as other players climb. Historically, the Legend list has been dominated by Japanese players, which says a lot about how strong the very top of the ladder is.

Seasons, Acts and resets

  • LP doesn't reset. A character that reaches Gold stays Gold; there is no season reset of the LP ladder.
  • MR runs in three-month periods. Capcom first called them Phases and now calls them Acts. They have started at the beginning of February, May, August and November.
  • The MR reset is soft. Since late 2024, your starting MR in a new Act is scaled from your final MR in the previous one, with the median kept at 1500. A player who finished high starts above 1500, one who finished low starts below it, and a character that didn't play the previous Act starts at 1500. Capcom's examples: 2200 became 1710, 1850 became 1605, 1200 became 1410. The idea is that new Masters don't get thrown against veterans on day one.
  • Master rewards. Playing ranked with a Master character during an Act earns points you can exchange for cosmetic rewards (titles, profile themes, character colors) in the Rewards menu. Each Act has its own set.

Reaching Master is a real milestone, but it's also a reset of expectations. At 1500 MR you're the newest Master in the pool, and the players around you have seen every common habit before. Many new Masters lose a lot of MR in their first weeks. That's the system finding your level, not a sign that you "didn't deserve" Master.

What to Expect at Each Part of the Ladder

This section is community wisdom, not statistics. Every rank has every kind of player, and you'll meet a Silver with perfect anti-airs and a Diamond who mashes on wakeup. But some patterns show up often enough that they're worth preparing for.

Rookie to Silver: learning to play the game, not the opponent

What you'll see: lots of jumping, random Drive Impacts thrown out from far away, long combos attempted from risky openers, unsafe specials on block, and players who never block low. Many opponents don't punish anything, so bad habits often go unpunished on both sides.

What to prioritize:

  • Anti-air consistently. At these ranks, a reliable anti-air wins more games than any combo. See Fundamentals.
  • Block, then punish the obvious. Learn which of your opponent's specials are unsafe and punish them with something simple. See Punish Finder.
  • Answer Drive Impact with Drive Impact. It is the single most common way players lose rounds here. See The Drive System.
  • One short, reliable combo from a light and one from a heavy. Not the longest combo: the one you land every time.

Gold to Platinum: the habits start to get punished

What you'll see: players who anti-air most of the time, who punish your unsafe specials, and who know one or two pressure loops for their character. Drive Rush pressure is common. The typical weaknesses are mashing when they're under pressure, autopilot habits (always the same move after a knockdown, always the same jump timing), and burning Drive Gauge until they're in Burnout.

What to prioritize:

  • Neutral and spacing. Pokes at the right range and whiff punishing. See Footsies.
  • Defense under pressure. Blocking instead of mashing, teching throws, knowing when you're actually allowed to press. Drive Gauge management becomes decisive. See The Drive System.
  • Basic offense: meaties, frame traps and throws after a knockdown. See Advanced Mechanics.
  • Watch your own losses. At this point, your losing patterns repeat. A few replays a week show them quickly.

Diamond: execution is no longer the excuse

What you'll see: most players have solid fundamentals and their character's main combos. The difference is in decisions. Opponents start to adapt during the set: they notice that you always jump after a fireball or always press after a blocked string, and they make you pay for it. Weaknesses at this level are usually narrower: a matchup they don't know, a specific situation they always answer the same way, or tilt after a lost round.

What to prioritize:

  • Adapting within a set. Noticing what the opponent does and changing your plan between rounds. See Advanced Strategy.
  • Matchup knowledge. Lab the specific things that beat you: a blockstring, a setup, a punish you missed. See Lab Exercises.
  • Optimal punishes and corner damage. At this level, the damage you leave on the table decides close rounds. See Punish Finder.
  • Emotional control. A Diamond player who tilts plays like a Gold player. See Psychology and Competitive Mindset.

Master: everyone has the basics

What you'll see: players who will exploit any habit you show twice. Conditioning, layered mixups, deep matchup knowledge and good defense are normal. The range inside Master is huge: a 1500 MR player and an Ultimate Master are not playing the same game.

What to prioritize:

  • Reducing your patterns. Review replays specifically looking for what you do every time, then add a second option. See Advanced Strategy.
  • Matchup depth for the characters you meet most often, built in the lab. See Lab Exercises.
  • Consistency over long sessions: the same quality in the twentieth match as in the first. See Psychology and Competitive Mindset.

The skills don't replace each other as you climb; they stack. Diamond players lose to bad anti-airs just like Silver players do. When you're stuck, go back down the list before looking for something new.

Other Ways to Play Online

Ranked is only one way to play against people. The other modes are often better for learning, because nothing is at stake and you can repeat the same matchup or the same situation.

Mode What it is What it's good for
Casual Match Online matchmaking without LP or MR Testing something new in real matches (a new combo, a new character, Modern instead of Classic) without worrying about your rank
Battle Hub The online lobby where you walk around with your avatar, sit at arcade cabinets to fight whoever sits across, watch other matches and join events Long sets against the same person, watching how stronger players play, and the social side of the game. Avatar battles and the arcade games are there too, but they're for fun, not for practicing your character
Custom Room A private or public room you create with your own rules, for one-on-one or for a group taking turns Playing friends, your local community or a sparring partner. The best place for long sets, focused practice ("I'll only work on anti-airs today") and small tournaments
Replays Your recent matches, available right from the match result screen ("Watch Replay") and in your profile; you can also look up other players' matches Seeing your losses from the outside. You can choose which round to watch, pause and go back

How to use replays without wasting time

  1. Pick the losses, not the wins. Choose two or three losses from the session, preferably close ones.
  2. Watch with one question. "Where did I take the most damage?" or "What did I do every time I got knocked down?" Watching without a question turns into reliving the match.
  3. Write down one pattern. One thing you did more than twice that lost you damage or your turn.
  4. Take it to the lab. If it's a situation you didn't know how to handle, recreate it in training mode. See Lab Exercises.

One practical detail: after a major game update, replays from the previous version can no longer be played back. If you find a replay worth studying, study it soon.

A simple split that works: use Casual Match or Custom Room when you're working on something new, and ranked when you want to see whether it holds up under pressure. Learning a new habit and being judged on it at the same time is the hardest way to learn anything.

Healthy Ranked Habits

The way you play ranked matters as much as how much you play. These habits come up again and again in advice from coaches and strong players.

Habits that help

  • Decide the session before you start. A number of matches or a time limit (for example, 10 to 15 matches or an hour), plus one skill to focus on.
  • Have a stop rule. For example: stop after three losses in a row, or as soon as you notice you're pressing buttons out of frustration. Come back later or switch to the lab.
  • Judge the session by the skill you chose. "I anti-aired 7 out of 10 jumps" is a good session even if you lost LP.
  • Rematch when you're learning something. Several games against the same opponent let you see their patterns and test your answers.
  • Keep your main for serious ranked. Alts are great for fun and for understanding other characters, but your main is where your improvements add up.

Habits that hurt

  • "One more game" after a loss, ten times in a row. Tilted games usually cost more LP than they win back.
  • Watching the LP number after every match. One match tells you almost nothing; a week tells you a little.
  • Rematching someone who is clearly beating you only because you're angry. If you aren't learning anything, decline and move on.
  • Switching characters after every bad session. Your problems usually travel with you to the new character.
  • Playing ranked when you're tired. Reaction-based skills (anti-air, Drive Impact answers, whiff punishes) are the first to go.

Tying ranked to the Progress Tracker

The Progress Tracker already has a line for your rank, but it works best when rank is the last line you look at, not the first. A practical weekly routine:

  1. Pick one or two skills for the week from the tracker (anti-air rate, punishing a specific move, matches played without tilting).
  2. During each ranked session, keep a simple tally of that skill, not of wins.
  3. At the end of the week, write down both your skill numbers and your rank.
  4. Compare over several weeks. If the skill numbers go up and the rank doesn't, keep going: rank lags behind skill. If the rank goes up and the skills don't, you may be winning on habits that will stop working higher up.

Example of a good session: 12 ranked matches with your main, focusing on anti-airs. Result: 5 wins, 7 losses, and a bit less LP than you started with. Anti-airs: 18 out of 26 jumps, up from about half last week. You stopped after the third loss in a row, watched two replays and noticed you always jump after a knockdown. That session is a step forward, even though the LP went down.

Ranked Myths

"My rank is my skill."

Rank reflects your results over many games, and those results come from your skills, your character knowledge, your mental state and a fair amount of luck in who you get matched with. Over hundreds of matches it's a decent estimate; over one evening it's mostly noise. Two players with the same rank can have very different strengths. Use rank as a rough direction, and use the specific skills in the Progress Tracker for the details.

"I need to change characters to rank up."

Sometimes a character really doesn't suit you, and switching to one you enjoy can help. But most of the time, what's holding a player back is universal: anti-air, defense, Drive Gauge management, decision-making. Those problems follow you to any character, and a new character also resets everything you knew about your old one. In the Legend rankings that have been analyzed, every character in the cast at the time had players in the top 500; the cast is balanced enough that your main is not what's keeping you in Gold.

"Modern is a shortcut" or "Modern is a handicap."

Modern gives easier access to special moves in exchange for fewer normals and reduced damage on specials done with the SP button. Both control types can climb the whole ladder. The control type you'll actually practice with is the right one.

"I'm stuck, so I'm not improving."

Plateaus are normal, and the steps between divisions get larger the higher you go. A player who improves while staying at the same rank is often just meeting better opponents as the ladder evens out. Check your skill numbers before deciding you're stuck.

"Matchmaking is rigged."

Long losing streaks feel personal, but they're exactly what happens with random matchmaking and a player who starts tilting. The fix for a bad streak is the stop rule, not more games. When you come back fresh, the streak usually ends on its own.

The one rule that matters most: ranked is a test, not the training. You get better in the lab, in focused sets and by reviewing your matches. Ranked tells you whether it's working. Play it with a plan, stop when you stop learning, and judge yourself by skills, not by LP.
