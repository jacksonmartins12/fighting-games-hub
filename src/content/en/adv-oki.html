The Mechanics chapter explains what a meaty is and why it matters. This chapter is the next step: working out, for your character and each of your knockdowns, which buttons can hit meaty, how long to wait before pressing them and how much advantage you end up with. It uses the numbers and the testing method from Reading and Measuring Frame Data.

Reading Knockdown Advantage

Frame data lists knockdowns as "KD +40" or "HKD +39". The number works like frame advantage: you can act 40 frames before your opponent. Count your first free frame as frame 1; their first frame awake is frame 41.

HKD (hard knockdown) doesn't allow back rise, so you know exactly where they'll stand up. After a normal knockdown they can back rise, which, as Knockdown & Okizeme explains, takes the same time but changes the distance.

Some entries carry more than one number, such as "KD +23*38" (different hits of the same move) or "KD +46(52)" (a variant). The calculator lists each value separately; the full frame data table in the character guide tells you which one applies.

The Meaty Formula

Four numbers decide a meaty

The knockdown advantage N, the frames you spend moving before you press the button D (a dash, walking), and the move's startup S and active frames A.

  • Pressed on frame p, a move is active from frame p + S − 1 to frame p + S + A − 2.
  • To hit meaty, that window must include frame N + 1, the first frame your opponent is awake.
  • The latest you can press is frame N + 2 − S. If you're still moving at that point (D is too big), the move is too slow for this setup.
  • Hitting on active frame k instead of the first one improves the move's on-block and on-hit numbers by k − 1.

Worked example: Akuma's forward throw

Goshoha (Akuma's forward throw) is KD +28, and his forward dash takes 19 frames, so after the dash you're free on frame 20. Crouch MK (8 frames of startup, 3 active) pressed right away is active on frames 27 to 29, and the opponent wakes up on frame 29. It hits on its third active frame: −6 on block becomes −4, and 0 on hit becomes +2. No waiting needed: throw, dash, press.

That's the calculation. Whether Crouch MK still reaches after the dash is something only training mode can tell you.

This example was also the test of the count. The formula assumes the opponent can be hit from frame N + 1, the first frame they can act. Tested in training mode on November 11, 2026: with Crouch MK pressed one frame before the dash ended, the input buffer brought it out right after the dash with no idle frame in between, and the frame meter showed −4 on block. The count is right: the opponent can be hit from frame N + 1 and not before. To repeat it, look for the best advantage on block with no gap between the dash and Crouch MK in the frame meter; −5 with no gap would have meant the opponent could be hit one frame earlier.

The same method shows when a setup is awkward. Ryu's Crouch HK is KD +40. After a forward dash he has 21 frames left, so a meaty Crouch MK means waiting 12 frames after the dash ends. Waiting 12 frames by feel won't be the same every time, which is why players fill that time with something of a fixed length.

Frame Kill: Filling the Extra Frames

Instead of waiting, spend the extra frames on something that always takes the same time, so the meaty lands on the same frame every time:

  • Walking forward. The calculator lets you add walking frames. It's the easiest to adjust and the hardest to repeat exactly.
  • A second dash, when the numbers allow it.
  • A whiffed normal with a known total length (startup + active + recovery), pressed out of range on purpose.
  • A projectile thrown while they're down, which also covers their wakeup from the front.

Whatever you use, add its length to the frames you spend and check that the meaty still lands where the calculator says.

Safe Meaties: Beating the Reversal Without Jumping

A safe jump isn't the only way to stay safe against an invincible reversal. A meaty on the ground can be safe too, if you recover fast enough to block the reversal after your attack goes through it. It works because the reversal's invincibility makes your meaty whiff: what matters is how long you stay stuck after that.

The rule, and where it comes from

Call stuck the frames you can't block after the meaty meets their first frame: the active frames left after the one that overlaps them, plus the move's recovery. If they press an invincible reversal on that first frame, your meaty whiffs through it, you can block again on frame first + stuck + 1, and their reversal becomes active on frame first + R − 1, where R is its startup. You block it if first + R − 1 ≥ first + stuck + 1, that is, stuck + 2 ≤ R.

dood.gg's rule of thumb is one frame more generous: stuck + 1 ≤ R (for a meaty on its last active frame, recovery + 1 ≤ R). The two would agree if the opponent could be hit one frame before they can act. The Akuma test in The Meaty Formula showed they can't: the first frame they can be hit is the first frame they can act, which is what the hub's count assumes. The calculator still marks the one-frame difference as a doubt until a safe meaty is tested directly against a recorded reversal.

Conditions: the reversal is pressed on its earliest frame and is invincible through your active frames; it can be blocked (a Super that grabs, like Zangief's SA3, can't, so no meaty is safe against it); and you hold block the moment you recover.

An example with this hub's data: Ryu's Stand LP has 7 frames of recovery, so, hitting on its last active frame, it's stuck for 7 frames. By the hub's count it's safe against reversals of 9 frames or slower (8 by dood.gg's rule), such as Luke's SA3 (10 frames). It isn't safe against a 6-frame reversal, and that's the startup of most of the cast's OD uppercuts and of Luke's SA1. dood.gg notes that no move in the game recovers fast enough to be safe against those, so against a 6-frame reversal the answers are a safe jump or blocking and baiting.

That's why the same meaty can be safe against one character and not another: it depends on their fastest invincible move, which you find in their frame data. The calculator below shows, for each meaty, how many frames you stay stuck after it hits, and checks it against a reversal startup you type in. One frame decides whether it's safe, so confirm every result with a recorded reversal in training mode.

Every character guide also has an "Oki and pressure, calculated" section that runs this calculation for all of the fighter's knockdowns at once: for each knockdown advantage, the normals that hit meaty with no wait after no dash, one dash or two, with their active frame, on-block and on-hit values and the reversal startup they're safe against.

What the Numbers Don't Check

  • Range. The calculator knows time, not distance. A perfectly timed meaty can still whiff because the dash took you too far or the knockdown left them too far away.
  • Back rise. After a normal knockdown they can back rise: same timing, more distance. Have a version of your setup for both.
  • Invincible reversals beat every meaty. Against a character with a fast invincible reversal, a meaty is a bet; Decisions by the Numbers shows how to price it.
  • Special cases. For multi-hit moves the calculator uses the first group of active frames, and it leaves out held, charged, stance, follow-up and air versions, and projectiles.

Test every setup before you use it in a match, following the testing protocol: record the dummy pressing a 4-frame normal on wakeup in one slot and a reversal in another, turn on the frame meter and check that your meaty hits on the frame you expected.

Training the Timing

A setup that works in training mode still has to come out under pressure. Practice it like a combo:

  • Find a cue. Tie the timing to something you can see or hear: the moment the dash ends, the sound of the opponent hitting the floor, a specific animation frame. A cue is easier to repeat than counting.
  • Twenty clean reps on each side, with the dummy set to block, so you hear whether the meaty connects on the frame you wanted.
  • Then with a recorded reversal and jab on random playback, so you practice reacting to what happens next, not only the setup.
  • Finally in matches, one setup at a time. Add the next one only when the first comes out without thinking.

Safe Jumps in Numbers

A safe jump is a meaty jump-in timed so that you land and can block before the opponent's invincible reversal becomes active. In frames, two things must be true:

  • The jumping attack is active when they wake up, so a late jump-in hits on their first frame.
  • You're back on the ground and able to block before their reversal becomes active.

To check one yourself:

  1. Look up your jump: "Jump Speed" on your character's SuperCombo data page, or measure it with the frame meter (how to measure).
  2. Add the first two numbers, pre-jump and air time. That's the knockdown advantage your safe jump needs: 4 + 38 = +42 for the standard jump. If the knockdown gives more, spend the extra frames first (a walk, a dash, a delay) so you jump with exactly that much left.
  3. Find the startup of the reversal you're worried about in that character's frame data. For most of the cast in SF6, that's an OD uppercut or a Super Art of 6 frames.
  4. Test it: record the dummy doing that reversal on wakeup. If you block it every time, the jump is safe. If it hits you, you're landing late; if your jump-in whiffs, you're early.

The same safe jump can work against one reversal and fail against a faster one, so note which reversals each safe jump covers.

All four fit one rule: the jump-in is safe when N + R ≥ J + 5, where J is pre-jump plus air time (42 for the standard jump). In the hub's count it means this: you press up on your first free frame (frame 1), the jump starts on frame 2, you're in the air until frame J + 1 and land on frames J + 2 to J + 4, so you can block from frame J + 5. Their reversal, pressed on their first frame (N + 1), becomes active on frame N + R. You block it if N + R ≥ J + 5. Your jump-in also has to meet their first frame while you're still in the air, so N + 1 ≤ J + 1: with more advantage than J, wait the extra frames before jumping.

The "jump starts the frame after the input" part is what the tests require, not something a source documents, so treat it as a fitted count. It predicts the rest of the cast's numbers: Zangief (J = 43) needs +43 against a 5-frame reversal and +42 against a 6-frame one; Chun-Li (J = 46) needs +46 and +45.

Why an empty jump at +41 still blocks the 5-frame reversal: without an attack, you can block during the landing frames, from frame J + 2.

Reproduce it: Luke's HP Flash Knuckle gives KD +42; jump forward at once and press HP as late as possible, with the dummy (Ryu) recording Light Shoryuken, then OD Shoryuken. For +41, use Sagat's HK Tiger Knee Crush, whose knockdown varies between +40 and +41: check that the frame meter shows +41 before trusting the result.

These tests used Ryu's reversals only. A reversal that's invincible only to air attacks, a counter, or one with a different hitbox can behave differently, so test the safe jump against the character you're facing.

Reference Numbers for Checking Your Math

These come from the SuperCombo wiki's movement page and from a Japanese safe jump guide. Use them to check your own measurements, not as a replacement for them:

  • Pre-jump: 4 frames for almost everyone; 5 for Lily and Zangief, 6 for C. Viper's High Jump. During these frames you can't be thrown, but strikes hit you as if you were on the ground.
  • In the air: 38 frames for the standard jump, so the whole jump takes 4 + 38 + 3 = 45 frames. Each character's jump is on their SuperCombo data page, under "Jump Speed", written as pre-jump + air + landing: 4+38+3 for most of the cast; the exceptions are Zangief 5+38+3, Lily 5+39+3, A.K.I. 4+40+3, Chun-Li 4+42+3, C. Viper 4+38+3 (6+40+3 for her High Jump), Dhalsim 4+68+3.
  • Landing: 3 frames. After an empty jump you can block and tech throws during them. After a jumping attack you can't block, crouch or tech, and getting hit there is a Punish Counter. That's why a safe jump has to land before the reversal: the jumping attack takes away your block on landing.
  • The +42 reference: for the standard jump, players use a knockdown of +42 for a safe jump with a jumping attack. Notice that 42 is the standard jump's pre-jump plus air time (4 + 38). Counting the same way, Zangief needs about +43, Lily and A.K.I. +44, Chun-Li +46 and C. Viper's High Jump +46, while Viper's normal jump uses the standard +42. Chun-Li's Crouch HK (+40) falls short, but in this hub's data her HK Hundred Lightning Kicks (+50), OD Hazanshu (+52) and OD Spinning Bird Kick (+55) leave room, with frames to spend first. Dhalsim would need about +72, and his own knockdowns in this hub's data reach +51 at most, so he has no forward jump safe jumps from them. In a training mode test, +42 with the standard jump blocked 5-frame reversals (Ryu's Light Shoryuken) and 6-frame ones, level 1 Supers included, while +41 with a jumping attack still blocked 6-frame OD reversals and Supers but was hit by a 5-frame reversal, such as Ryu's Light Shoryuken. Drez Zero's safe jump guide calls this a "pseudo safe jump" and shows the same thing with Sagat's HK Tiger Knee Crush (KD +41). Each frame below your number costs you the fastest reversal it used to cover. The same jump at +41 without an attack (an empty jump) blocked the 5-frame reversal too, because only after an empty jump can you block during the landing frames.
  • Where it pays off: in the corner. Midscreen a back rise puts them farther away, so the same setup may not reach, and a jump that lands too far gives them time. Combos that carry the opponent toward the corner are the ones worth ending in a safe jump. Faster reversals leave less room, so test against the one you're facing.
  • Empty jump: for most characters, KD +44 sets up an empty jump into a 4f normal, and KD +45 an empty jump into a throw or a 5f normal.
  • Dashes cost frames: everything you do before jumping comes out of the knockdown advantage. With a 19f dash, the most common forward dash, a safe jump after one dash needs about +61, and two dashes from +42 leave only +4: enough for a fast meaty, not for a jump. The calculator above does this subtraction for you.

  2. In the calculator, find a button that hits meaty without waiting, or add a frame kill until one does.
