Structured Training Regimens

UniversalThe ideas in this chapter apply to any fighting game. Examples come from SF6, and sections marked SF6 only apply to it. For how other games differ, see Fighting Game Families.

Random practice leads to random results. Structured training regimens accelerate improvement by focusing your effort. Here are sample weekly plans at different skill levels.

Deliberate Practice: What Makes Training Work

The regimens below follow the idea of deliberate practice, studied for decades by psychologist Anders Ericsson and explained in Peak (written with Robert Pool, 2016). His main finding: what separates experts from everyone else isn't how many hours they put in, but what kind of practice fills those hours. In chess, for example, the best predictor of a player's strength isn't the number of games played but the time spent alone studying grandmaster games.

"I Just Played It"

Ericsson's favorite example of practice that doesn't work is Benjamin Franklin's chess. Franklin played for more than fifty years, often through the night and sometimes against the best player of his time, and never got much past above average. He just played; he never pushed beyond what he could already do. Hours of ranked on autopilot are the same thing. The book sums it up with a music teacher asking a student who didn't improve: how many times did you play the piece? "Ten or twenty." How many times did you get it right? "Once or twice." How did you practice? "I don't know. I just played it."

Naive Practice

Three hours of ranked with no goal

Repeating a combo you already land every time

No feedback beyond win or lose

→ Lots of hours, little improvement

Purposeful Practice

A specific goal: anti-air 8 of 10 jumps from far away

Full focus, in shorter sessions

Immediate feedback: frame meter, input display, replays

→ Just outside your comfort zone, steady improvement

The Four Traits of Purposeful Practice

  • A specific goal: not "get better at defense" but "block the overhead in this string 8 times out of 10". Break the big goal into small steps you can check.
  • Focus: you rarely improve without your full attention. Swimmer Natalie Coughlin only became great when she stopped daydreaming during laps and used them to perfect each stroke.
  • Feedback: know right away whether you got it right, and if not, why. SF6 gives you plenty: the frame meter, the input display, combo trials and replays.
  • Outside your comfort zone: always a little beyond what you can already do. Without that, experience alone doesn't help: research Ericsson cites found doctors with twenty or thirty years in practice doing worse on some objective measures than doctors two or three years out of school.

Mental Representations: What Experts See

Ericsson's chess research showed that masters don't calculate faster; they recognize patterns. Where a beginner sees twenty pieces, a master sees a few familiar structures and already knows what matters. Herbert Simon estimated that a master has some fifty thousand of these patterns stored. Ericsson calls them mental representations, and building them is, in his view, the main point of practice.

SF6 works the same way. A beginner sees "Ken walked forward". An experienced player sees "Ken has three Drive bars, he's just outside his 2MK range, and last time he Drive Rushed from here", and knows what to do before Ken presses anything. That's why experienced players seem to react faster: often they're anticipating, not reacting.

In one of Ericsson's studies, soccer players watched match footage that stopped just as a player received the ball and had to predict what would happen next; the better players predicted much better. You can train this the same way: pause a replay right before the opponent acts and guess what they'll do. Representations come from doing, not just thinking: watch, try to reproduce it, fail, find out why, try again.

The 3 Fs: Focus, Feedback, Fix

The book's summary for practicing without a teacher:

  • Focus: full attention on one thing. Practicing while watching a stream or chatting is mostly wasted time.
  • Feedback: know right away whether you got it right: the frame meter, the input display, replays or a stronger player watching.
  • Fix: use that feedback to change the next attempt instead of just repeating it. If it still isn't working, change the approach: slow down, split the combo into parts, change the drill.

Break the skill into parts you can repeat and analyze one at a time.

The Top Gun Approach

During the Vietnam War, the U.S. Navy created the Top Gun school to improve its fighter pilots. The best pilots stayed on as instructors and flew as the enemy in simulated dogfights, which the students usually lost at first. The real learning came after landing, in the after-action reviews: what did you notice up there? What did you do, and why? What were your mistakes? What could you have done differently? Over time, the students learned to ask themselves those questions. Pilots could fail without paying the real price, then find out exactly why they failed. Ericsson sees that as a recipe for any field: recreate the real situation, repeat it with plenty of feedback, and review it afterward. The fighting game version is training mode with a recorded dummy doing what beats you, followed by a replay review that asks yourself those same questions.

Getting Past a Plateau

A plateau usually means you've settled into what you already do. Ericsson's rule: the answer is usually not to try harder but to try differently. In his memory study, Steve Faloon got stuck several times, and each time he got past it by changing his technique, not by repeating the same thing.

When nothing works, find out exactly what's holding you back. A complex skill has many parts, and usually only one or two are failing. To find them, push yourself a little harder than usual and see what breaks first; typing teachers do it by having students type 10 to 20% faster than normal for 15 to 20 minutes a day. In SF6, play people stronger than you, or set the dummy to punish faster, and pay attention to what goes wrong when things get chaotic: those errors were there all along, just hidden. Then build a drill for that one thing: not "my defense is bad" but "I get Drive Rush thrown after my 2MK is blocked".

Practicing Without a Teacher: Emulate and Compare

Benjamin Franklin, the same one who never improved at chess, taught himself to write by taking notes on articles he admired, rewriting them from his notes a few days later and comparing his version with the original to see what he'd missed. That's exactly how chess players study: they replay grandmaster games, try to guess each move and, when they guess wrong, go back to see what they missed. Franklin couldn't do that with chess because he had no access to the masters' games. You can: pause a top player's replay at a decision point, decide what you would do, then watch what they did. Every difference is something to learn. Lab Exercise 8 turns this into a routine.

Finding a Coach

  • The coach needs to be ahead of you. A teacher can usually take you only as far as they or their students have gone. For a beginner, any solid player will do; later you'll need someone stronger.
  • Playing well isn't the same as teaching well. Look at how much the students improved, especially those who started at your level, not at how fun the sessions are.
  • Ask what to practice between sessions. Most of your improvement comes from practicing alone, and a good coach teaches you to spot your own mistakes.
  • Good teachers balance praise and correction and have patience with beginners (Leonard, in the Progression chapter, makes the same point).
  • When you stop improving, it's fine to change coaches. You may have learned everything this one can teach.

Staying Motivated

Ericsson doesn't believe in "willpower" as a fixed trait: it varies from one activity to another, and calling yourself someone without willpower only gives you a reason to quit. He prefers to think of motivation as a balance between reasons to keep going and reasons to stop.

  • Weaken the reasons to stop: a fixed practice time with nothing else scheduled, phone in another room, Discord muted.
  • Keep sessions to about an hour. In his view, nobody holds intense concentration much longer than that, and beginners hold it for less. To train longer, take a break every hour. Better 100% effort for less time than 70% for longer; when you can't focus anymore, end the session.
  • Sleep well: the best violin students in his study slept more than the merely good ones, mostly thanks to afternoon naps.
  • Strengthen the reasons to keep going: a training group, friends who understand the effort, the pride of seeing yourself improve.
  • Make a deal with yourself. When you regress or plateau and want to quit, first get back to where you were or past the plateau, then decide. Ericsson notes that you probably won't quit.

Focused Practice Isn't Always Fun

The book is honest about this: deliberate practice is hard work and usually not fun. In one study of singing lessons, amateurs left the lesson elated, while professionals left having concentrated on technique, with focus but no joy. The best spelling bee contestants didn't enjoy studying either; they were just better at sticking with it. Over time, practice gets closer to neutral. That doesn't contradict Leonard's love of the plateau: you can love the path even when some steps are hard. Playing for fun has its place too, as long as you don't mistake it for practice (see the Practice Mindset in the Psychology chapter).

The "10,000-Hour Rule" Is a Misreading

The popular "10,000 hours to become an expert" came from one of Ericsson's studies, and he spent years correcting it. Ten thousand was the average for the best violin students in that study by age 20, and half of them hadn't reached it; they were also still students, not masters (international piano competition winners are closer to 20,000 or 25,000 hours by around age 30). The number changes from field to field, and it counted solitary, goal-directed practice. Performing isn't practice: hours of ranked, like a band's hours on stage, don't count the same way. What the rule gets right is that getting really good takes many years of effort. Ericsson's more important message is the other side of it: no one has found a ceiling on how much people can improve with the right training. The book also argues against innate talent as the deciding factor: early advantages fade, and what keeps separating players over the years is how they practice.

Beginner Regimen (Months 1-2): Building Foundation

Goal: Develop Consistent Execution & Game Familiarity

Time Commitment: 9-10 hours/week

Weekly Breakdown

  • Mon/Wed/Fri (Training Mode - 1 hr each):
    • 5 min: Character select and combo tool intro
    • 10 min: Basic movement (walk, dash, jump, block) and anti-airing dummy jump-ins
    • 15 min: BnB (Bread & Butter) combo on dummy from neutral
    • 20 min: Combo on standing/crouching/airborne dummies
    • 10 min: Throw practice (walk-up throw, throw tech, empty jump into throw)
  • Tue/Thu (Casual Play - 1.5 hrs each):
    • 10 min: Warm-up combos (muscle memory)
    • 40 min: CPU matches (Level 3-4)
    • 30 min: Casual online (5-6 matches)
    • 10 min: Reflection (write 2-3 notes on what happened)
  • Sat/Sun (Practice + Social):
    • Sat: 1-2 hours free play (mix training/CPU/casual)
    • Sun: 2 hours community play if available, otherwise casual online

Intermediate Regimen (Months 3-6): Mastering Footsies

Goal: Develop Footsies Mastery & Pattern Recognition

Time Commitment: 10-15 hours/week

Weekly Breakdown

  • Mon/Wed/Fri (Focused Lab - 1.5 hrs each):
    • 15 min: Execution warmup (combos, special moves)
    • 30 min: Footsies position training (record the training dummy, play against the recordings)
    • 30 min: Anti-air reactions (jump-in patterns, crossup defense)
    • 15 min: Okizeme setup practice (specific knockdown scenarios)
  • Tue/Thu (Ranked Play - 2 hrs each):
    • 10 min: Warmup
    • 90 min: Ranked matches (focus on patterns, not W/L)
    • 20 min: Replay analysis (watch 2-3 of your losses)
  • Saturday (Matchup Study - 2 hrs):
    • Watch pro player VOD (your character vs common opponent)
    • Take notes on patterns, optimal spacing, tech choices
    • Play 1 hour against player using that character
  • Sunday (Community + Reflection - 3 hrs):
    • 2 hours: Local community play (if available)
    • 1 hour: Post-play analysis and journal writing

Advanced Regimen (Month 7+): Competitive Refinement

Goal: Adapt Strategy, Master Adaptation, Prepare for Competition

Time Commitment: 15-20 hours/week (if competing)

Weekly Breakdown

  • Daily Warmup (30 min):
    • Combo practice (5 min)
    • Movement drills (5 min)
    • Anti-air reactions (10 min)
    • Quick CPU match (10 min)
  • Mon/Wed/Fri (Specific Matchup Lab - 2 hrs each):
    • 30 min: Replay analysis (your recent losses)
    • 60 min: Practice against difficult matchup (CPU + online)
    • 30 min: Research (watch pro, take notes, test new tech)
  • Tue/Thu (Ranked + Recording - 2.5 hrs each):
    • 2.5 hours: Ranked/casual play (or tournament if available)
    • Record for post-analysis
  • Sat/Sun (Community + Teaching - 4 hrs total):
    • Local tournaments or community events
    • Teach newcomers (solidifies your knowledge)
    • Post-event analysis
