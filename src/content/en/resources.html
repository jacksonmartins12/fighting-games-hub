Essential Learning Resources

You don't need to reinvent the wheel. The fighting game community has created excellent free resources. Here's what to watch, read and use at each stage of your journey. Channels and tools change over time, so if a link has moved, search for the creator's name.

Video Creators Worth Knowing

Educational YouTube Channels

  • Core-A Gaming: Widely considered the gold standard for fighting game analysis. Clear, well-produced videos on footsies, frame data, game design and fighting game psychology. A great starting point at any level.
  • Brian_F: Practical, beginner-friendly Street Fighter 6 guides covering systems, character basics and how to practice. Often recommended to new SF6 players by other well-known creators.
  • gootecks: Long-time Street Fighter educator (Cross Counter) focused on fundamentals and practice routines, and author of the "Simplifying Street Fighter" guides.
  • Sajam: Fighting game commentary and discussion of game systems, patch changes and the community. Good for understanding the "why" behind the meta.
  • Rooflemonger and Bafael: Detailed breakdowns of SF6 mechanics and system design. Best once you know the basics and want to understand the game more deeply.

Frame Data and Lab Tools

  • dood.gg: Japanese SF6 database, also in English and Portuguese. Move search across all characters, punishes and invincible moves per character, the situation after every throw and every invincible reversal, and short guides on frame data, safe meaties, fuzzy guard and option selects.
  • SuperCombo Wiki (wiki.supercombo.gg): Community wiki with frame data, move properties and combos for Street Fighter 6 and many other games. The main reference for SF frame data.
  • Dustloop Wiki: The equivalent wiki for Arc System Works games such as Guilty Gear and Granblue Fantasy Versus. Use it if you play those games, not for Street Fighter.
  • SF6 training mode frame meter: SF6's training mode can display a frame meter showing startup, active frames, recovery and advantage in real time. It's the fastest way to check whether something is safe.
  • Frame Assistant Tool (FAT): A mobile app with frame data for several fighting games, handy for quick look-ups between matches.
  • FOOTSIES by HiFight (hifight.github.io/footsies): A free, minimalist fighting game that strips everything down to spacing, whiff punishing and hit-confirming. An excellent way to practice pure footsies.
  • The Fighting Game Glossary by Infil (glossary.infil.net): Over 800 fighting game terms with video examples and Japanese names. The reference when you meet a term you don't know.

Tournament VODs and Pro Play

  • Major tournaments (EVO, Combo Breaker, CEO, Capcom Pro Tour events): Usually streamed on Twitch and archived on YouTube. Watch top players who use your character and write down what they do differently from you.
  • Character-specific streamers: Find players who main your character and watch their streams. Many answer questions in chat.
  • Local tournament VODs: Your local scene's top players are the people you'll actually face, and their habits are often more relevant to you than world-class play.

Learning Path by Phase

Month 1-2: Foundations

Watch: Core-A Gaming's videos on footsies and frame data; a Brian_F beginner guide for your game.

Read: From Masher to Master (Patrick Miller).

Play: Casual and ranked matches, focusing on getting comfortable with the game.

Time: 3-5 hours watching and reading in total.

Month 3-4: Intermediate

Watch: More Core-A Gaming analysis; pro VODs of your character.

Read: Street Fighter Footsies Handbook and Playing to Win.

Practice: Play some FOOTSIES (HiFight) to isolate spacing and whiff punishing.

Play: Ranked seriously; study 1-2 character matchups.

Month 5+: Advanced

Watch: Top players of your character in tournament VODs, plus system breakdowns from Rooflemonger or Bafael.

Read: Matchup notes in your character's Discord; SuperCombo pages for your hardest matchups.

Play: Local tournaments and community events.

Don't Overwhelm Yourself

The Resource Trap

You can easily spend 10 hours watching videos for every hour you play. That's backwards.

Healthy ratio: roughly 80% play, 20% study. Watch 30 minutes of content, then play for a couple of hours. This keeps your learning grounded in actual gameplay.

Passive watching feels productive but often isn't. Watch with a specific gap to fill (e.g. "I lose to crossups" → watch one video on crossup defense, then lab it for an hour).
