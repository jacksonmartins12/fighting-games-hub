3D Fighters: Fighting in Depth

FamiliesAn overview of how other fighting games work, written for SF6 players. Systems in other games also change with patches and sequels, so confirm details in each game's own training mode or wiki.

Tekken, Virtua Fighter, Soulcalibur and Dead or Alive share everything in The Path: spacing, frame advantage, reads, conditioning, practice. What changes is the shape of the fight. Characters can step into and out of the background, the main mixup is mid against low instead of low against overhead, and jumping is rarely a way to approach. This chapter explains those differences from the point of view of an SF6 player.

The Third Axis: Sidesteps and Tracking

In SF6 there are two ways to avoid an attack: block it or be out of its range. In a 3D game there's a third: step to the side. Moves are either linear, travelling straight ahead, or tracking, covering some sideways movement. A sidestep makes a linear move whiff, and a whiffed move can be punished just like in SF6.

How Movement Becomes Defense

  • Sidestep: a short step into or out of the screen. In Tekken you can keep walking sideways (sidewalk). In Virtua Fighter it's called an evade, done by tapping up or down at the right moment; some attacks can't be evaded.
  • Tracking moves hit you even if you step, usually to one side only. Moves that track to both sides are called homing moves in Tekken. They're the answer to an opponent who steps too much, the same way a throw is the answer to someone who only blocks.
  • Soulcalibur takes this further with 8-way run: you can run freely in eight directions, and horizontal attacks (A) catch sideways movement, while vertical attacks (B) are linear and lose to it.
  • Backdashing matters too. Tekken players cancel backdashes to cover distance quickly (the Korean backdash), which plays the role that walking back plays in SF6 footsies.

This creates a new guessing triangle on top of the usual one: linear attack beats someone who blocks or attacks, sidestep beats a linear attack, tracking attack beats the sidestep.

High, Mid and Low: Blocking Turned Around

In SF6 you crouch-block by default, because lows are common and overheads are slow. In 3D games the default flips. Most attacks are mids, which hit crouching opponents, so the default is to block standing. The defender's guess is mid against low:

Attack heightBlocked byWhat happens if you crouch
HighStandingIt whiffs over your head, and you can punish it
MidStandingIt hits you
LowCrouchingYou block it, often with a big punish afterwards

Lows usually do little damage and are often very unsafe when blocked, so the attacker mixes a few lows into a mostly mid offense. The defender stands, watches for the low animations and crouches on reaction or on a read. Ducking a high and punishing with a move done while rising is a core 3D skill with no direct SF6 equivalent. Tekken adds a low parry (down-forward at the right time) that knocks some lows away entirely.

Frame Data the 3D Way

The logic of frame advantage is exactly the same as in SF6: if you block a move and recover enough frames before the attacker, you punish. The conventions differ. Tekken lists startup as "i10" for a 10-frame move, and the community thinks in thresholds:

On blockWhat it means in Tekken
0 to −9Safe. The defender can't punish and usually has to respect the attacker's next move
−10Punishable by a jab: small damage, but the turn changes hands
−12 to −14Stronger punishes, often a knockdown
−15 or worseLaunch punishable: most characters get a full combo

Two more differences. Blocked lows leave you crouching, so you punish them with moves that come out of a crouch, which are different moves from your standing punishers. And because each Tekken character has a very large move list, players learn the key unsafe moves of each opponent rather than everything they can do. The method is the same as Lab Exercise 6: record the move, block it and test your fastest options.

The Ground, Walls and Ring Outs

Being Knocked Down in 3D

In SF6 a knockdown leads to a fairly fixed wakeup: you rise in place or back rise. In 3D games the downed player has many more options: stay on the ground, roll back or to the side, tech-roll the moment you land (ukemi, P+K+G in Virtua Fighter), or attack while getting up. The attacker in turn has ground hits that only work on a downed opponent. Okizeme becomes a guessing game about what the defender does on the ground, not only about timing a meaty.

Walls and Ring Outs

  • Walls play the role of the corner. Hitting an opponent into a wall stops them and allows big combos; in Tekken 8 some walls and floors break and open new combo routes.
  • Ring outs exist in Soulcalibur and on some Virtua Fighter stages: push the opponent off the edge and you win the round outright, whatever the life bars say. Position becomes a win condition, not just an advantage.

Throws and Throw Breaks

Throws still beat blocking, but escaping them works differently in each series. Instead of one universal tech button, the defender often has to identify the throw:

  • Tekken: watch which arm reaches out. A throw led by the left arm is broken with 1, by the right arm with 2, and with both arms together with 1+2. Many command throws have their own breaks.
  • Virtua Fighter: press P+G with the direction of the opponent's throw input, so the escape is a guess among directions.
  • Dead or Alive: built around a triangle. Strikes beat throws, throws beat holds, and holds (counters that catch strikes) beat strikes. Catching the opponent in the middle of the option that loses gives bonus damage.

The Main 3D Series at a Glance

Tekken 8

Four limbs, one button each (1, 2, 3, 4). The Heat system can be activated once per round for a 10-second power-up, by Heat Burst (2+3) or by landing a character's Heat Engager move. Damage leaves a recoverable part of the life bar that you win back by landing attacks, even blocked ones. Rage Art becomes available below 25% health. Huge move lists, sidesteps and wall combos.

Virtua Fighter 5

Punch, Kick and a dedicated Guard button. Evades, throw escapes by direction, ring outs on some stages. Known for very precise frame situations and a strong focus on fundamentals: the series rewards knowing exactly whose turn it is.

Soulcalibur VI

Weapons, 8-way run, horizontal (A) and vertical (B) attacks, Guard Impact as a timed parry, Reversal Edge as a slow-motion guessing game, Soul Charge as a temporary power-up and Critical Edge as a super. Ring outs make stage position decisive.

Dead or Alive 6

Fast strikes and a hold system: the defender can catch an incoming strike with the right hold. The strike, throw and hold triangle is the center of every interaction, with 150% damage for catching the opponent in the losing option.

A First Week Plan for a 3D Game

  • Learn your character's 10 to 15 key moves, not the whole list: a fast jab, a fast mid, a low, a launcher, a tracking move and your main punishers.
  • Practice standing block and crouching only on lows you recognize.
  • Learn your punishers for −10, −12 and −15, standing and crouching.
  • Spend a session only sidestepping a recorded linear attack and punishing the whiff.
  • Look up the community wiki for your game before trusting what you remember from SF6.
