Decisions by the Numbers

UniversalThe math works in any fighting game. The examples use Street Fighter 6 damage from this hub's frame data.

Every mixup and every read is a bet. Risk and Reward showed one example, and Thinking in Bets explained why a good bet can still lose. This chapter turns that into a routine: put numbers on your options, estimate how often your opponent does each thing, and mix your own choices so you stay hard to read.

How Often You Need to Be Right

If an option gains G when it works and costs L when it fails, it only pays off when it works more than L / (G + L) of the time. Gaining 1,400 and risking 3,000 needs about 68%; gaining 2,000 and risking 1,000 needs only about 33%.

Use damage you've measured in training mode, not guesses: your punish combo, their punish combo, your throw. And health isn't the only thing at stake. Drive bars, meter and screen position count too: an option that leaves you cornered costs more than its damage.

Where the Numbers Come From

  • Damage: training mode shows the damage of every combo. Measure your punishes, your throw, your best meaty combo and the opponent's main punish, midscreen and in the corner.
  • Resources: the frame data lists Drive and Super Art costs; count them as part of the price of each option.
  • Their habits: your own replays (in the game or on Buckler's Boot Camp, Capcom's SF6 site) and the tallies from your replay reviews.

Why You Shouldn't Always Pick the Best Option

If throwing is your best option against a player who blocks a lot and you throw every time, they start teching, and your best option becomes your worst. In a mixup, what matters isn't any single choice but how often you make each one. Game theory calls this a mixed strategy: you choose your frequencies so that no single answer from the opponent beats all of them.

  • Start from their habit. If they tech most of the time, shimmy and strike more; if they mostly block, throw more.
  • Change before they do. When an option starts working too well, they're about to adapt. That's the moment for the option that beats their adaptation (the yomi layers in Strategy).
  • Keep a little of everything. Even an option that's wrong against their current habit stops them from settling into one answer.

Reading Habits as Percentages

The replay review asks you to write reads as odds. Here's how to get them: in your next sets, note what the opponent did each time they woke up next to you, whether they blocked, teched, pressed a button, jumped or used a reversal. After ten knockdowns you have percentages. They're rough, but much better than "he always mashes".

Example: ten wakeups

The opponent blocked 5 times, teched 3 times, pressed a button once and used a reversal once.

Your optionWinsNothing happensLoses
Meaty strike4 of 10 (tech and button)5 of 10 (blocked, you keep the turn)1 of 10 (reversal)
Throw5 of 10 (block)3 of 10 (teched)2 of 10 (button and reversal)

Against this player both options win often. The one reversal is what makes the strike risky, and the button is what makes the throw risky. Put your damage in the calculator to decide which one to favor, and keep using both.

Defense Is a Bet Too

Use the same math for your escapes. Ryu's OD Shoryuken deals 1,600 and is −40 on block. If a blocked one costs you around 3,000, you need to be right about 65% of the time, and you also spend 2 Drive bars. That's why the usual default on wakeup is to block and read, and to reversal only when the opponent's pattern justifies it.

Across a Set

Frequencies change during a set. The first round is for seeing their habits; the next ones are for exploiting them (see Resource Management). In game two, expect them to change whatever cost them the most in game one, and be ready with the option that beats that change. After each set, write down their percentages and your own in the replay review.

Try This in Your Next Session

  1. Count ten of your opponent's wakeups next to you: block, tech, button, jump, reversal.
  2. Turn them into percentages and fill in a table like the one above.
  3. Put your measured damage into the calculator and decide on a mix, for example "throw 5, strike 4, shimmy 1".
  4. Play the next set with that mix and count again. If their numbers changed, they adapted, and so should you.
