Core systems

Build a reliable foundation with defense, stable combos, Drive Gauge management, and repeatable okizeme.

How to use the Drive Gauge
Drive Impact

An armored strike that is especially threatening near the corner. It loses to a timely counter-DI, throws, and suitable multi-hit attacks.

Drive Parry

A defensive stance for receiving attacks. Use it against projectiles and to create Perfect Parry opportunities.

Drive Rush

A fast approach that enhances the next normal. Use raw Drive Rush to enter pressure or Cancel Drive Rush to extend a combo.

OD Special Moves

Enhanced special moves that spend Drive Gauge. Their benefits differ by move, including damage, invincibility, and corner carry.

Drive Reversal

A defensive option that pushes the opponent away and can interrupt sustained pressure.

Burnout

The state caused by depleting Drive Gauge. Defensive options are weaker, so manage Drive resources before committing.

How to read frame data

Use frame values to shortlist an answer, then test spacing, pushback and contact timing in Training Mode. CAPCOM's official Frame Data remains the source of truth for current numerical values.

What each frame value tells you

SF6 Lab explains how to use the values. CAPCOM’s official Frame Data remains the source of truth for the current numbers.

STARTUP
Startup

The number of frames before the move becomes active. Compare it with the opponent’s recovery only after identifying the exact move strength and state.

ACTIVE
Active frames

The period in which the attack can hit. A later active frame can change the resulting advantage, so the table value alone does not describe every contact.

RECOVERY
Recovery

The time before the attacker can act again after the active portion ends. Whiff recovery and contact recovery should not be treated as the same situation.

ADVANTAGE
On hit / on block

A positive or negative value shows who can act first after contact. It does not guarantee that the next move reaches.

Why a punish can be fast enough but still fail

Frame advantage narrows the candidates. Spacing, pushback, active-frame contact, cancel routes and your own input decide whether the response works in practice.

  • Did I select the correct strength and state?
  • Did the move contact close or at tip range?
  • Can the move cancel or continue into another action?
  • Does my response reach from the recorded spacing?
  • Can I reproduce the input at match speed?
A repeatable Training Mode check

Use the same process for every fighter instead of memorizing a table as a complete answer.

01
Record the exact move

Record the strength, resource state and follow-up you actually want to test.

02
Test more than one spacing

Compare close contact with tip range instead of accepting one successful punish.

03
Compare candidate responses

Try moves with different startup and reach. Record both why a response worked and why it failed.

04
Keep one match-ready answer

Choose a response you can reproduce, not only the highest theoretical reward.

FGC glossary

32 terms used in guides, commentary, frame-data discussions, and training mode.

Neutral & Defense
Neutral
The phase where neither player is locked into pressure and both compete for space.
Footsies
Grounded movement and attacks used to control space and provoke a whiff.
Poke
A relatively safe button used to control a specific range.
Whiff punish
Hitting the recovery of an attack that missed.
Anti-air
An attack used to stop an opponent approaching from the air.
Cross-up
A jumping attack that lands on the opposite side and changes the required block direction.
Overhead
An attack that must be blocked standing.
Reversal
An action performed on the first available frame after knockdown, blockstun, or hitstun.
Frames & Hits
Frame
The basic unit of time in a fighting game; SF6 runs at 60 frames per second.
Startup
Frames before an attack becomes active.
Active
Frames during which an attack can hit.
Recovery
Frames after the active period before the character can act again.
Frame advantage
The difference in how soon each character can act after a hit or block.
Counter Hit
A hit that interrupts the startup or active frames of an opponent’s action.
Punish Counter
A hit that catches the punishable recovery of an opponent’s action.
True blockstring
A sequence with no gap in which the defender remains in blockstun.
Drive System
Drive Gauge
The shared resource used for Drive system actions and OD special moves.
Drive Impact
An armored strike that can cause a wall splat near the corner.
Drive Parry
A defensive stance that parries incoming attacks while held.
Perfect Parry
A precisely timed Drive Parry that creates a punish opportunity with damage scaling.
Drive Rush
A fast green dash from parry or from a cancellable normal.
Cancel Drive Rush
A Drive Rush cancelled from a cancellable normal attack.
Drive Reversal
A defensive Drive action used to push the opponent away.
Burnout
The state entered when the Drive Gauge is depleted.
OD Special Move
An enhanced special move that consumes Drive Gauge.
Okizeme & Offense
Okizeme
Offense applied as the opponent gets up from a knockdown.
Meaty
An attack timed so its later active frames connect during the opponent’s wake-up.
Shimmy
Walking into throw range, then stepping back to punish a throw tech attempt.
Throw tech
Escaping a throw by inputting throw during the tech window.
Throw loop
A situation where a throw leads back into another close throw/strike mix-up.
Frame trap
A sequence with a small gap designed to counter-hit an opponent who presses a button.
Setplay
A repeatable offensive sequence built around a specific knockdown and timing.

Articles From SF6 Lab

Four practice articles by Iori, each built on one of his videos. SF6 Lab has them only in Japanese; this hub translated them into English.

Focus practice: watching for one thing at a time (Cammy matchup)

Material: Iori's Cammy matchup video. The Cammy part is about the first 3 minutes 30 seconds. After listing her ways of getting in, it shows narrowing down what you watch for instead of watching everything, and checking with your own character what to do after you stop her. (Video in Japanese.)

1. Once you've listed every way she gets in, narrow down what you watch

Video 0:12 to 0:33

What happens in the video

The video lines up the several actions Cammy uses to make contact: Cannon Strike, Quick Spin Knuckle, Drive Rush, walking, and others.

What Iori teaches

His conclusion: don't try to answer all of them at once; first narrow down the action you want to answer. Knowing how many options exist and watching all of them in one match are separate things.

Practice to try

Before a match, write one sentence: "this time I watch only for this action". Not starting a match without a target is the first condition of this practice.

2. When an action you weren't watching gets through, accept it for now

Video 0:34 to 0:52

What happens in the video

It assumes that, with your attention on one action, another approach gets through. If you change your target every time that happens, you end up reacting to nothing again.

What Iori teaches

When an action you didn't choose gets through, you also need to accept it as "it can't be helped". That avoids freezing up from trying to react to everything.

Practice to try

Don't keep changing your target during a match. Afterward, judge whether you reacted to the target you chose at the start, not the action you lost to.

3. Pair the action you watch with the action you answer with

Video 0:54 to 1:22

What happens in the video

The video demonstrates watching for Quick Spin Knuckle and watching for Cannon Strike separately, with a different way of waiting and a different answer for each.

What Iori teaches

Besides choosing the target, pair it with "what I press when I see it". This article doesn't guarantee that the specific numbers and interactions from when the video was published still hold; it uses only the idea of preparing target and answer one to one.

Practice to try

Play the target action back in training mode and decide on a single answer. When it works, repeat the same pair; don't mix in another answer.

4. Don't learn sequences by name: test how to stop them with your character

Video 2:22 to 2:52

What happens in the video

Iori reproduces a specific Cammy sequence, inserts his own character's action in the middle, and checks where it can be stopped.

What Iori teaches

Don't stop at reading matchup information: record the opponent's action and reproduce the same situation with your character. Don't use an old video's numbers as they are; check with what the game shows now.

Practice to try

Put the opponent's whole sequence in one recording slot. Play only that recording at first, and note separately whether you failed on input, distance or timing.

5. Practice the reward after you react, too

Video 2:53 to 3:29

What happens in the video

It compares stopping the target action with a single light attack against having the next action prepared as well.

What Iori teaches

Don't stop at "I saw it": find out what your character does after stopping it. Record the success of the reaction and the success of the input after it separately.

Practice to try

First practice only stopping the target; once that's stable, add one next action. If the reaction itself falls apart, remove what you added and go back a step.

Building a "waiting drill" in training mode

If you mix several recordings from the start, you can't tell why you failed to react. In these six steps, add only one condition at a time.

  1. Choose one target. Pick a single action that keeps troubling you in matches. The example here is Cammy, but put it in words as "I watch only for this movement", not as a move name.
  2. Record only the target action on the dummy. Keep the distance and starting position the same. At first, don't mix in other approaches; learn how it looks and its timing.
  3. Decide on one answer. Check what the game shows now and your character's moves, and fix the input. Don't choose again among several candidates each time.
  4. Record recognition and input separately. Did you not see it, or did you see it and fail the input? Not lumping failures together decides the next practice.
  5. Add a single non-target recording. Play the target and one action you're giving up this time at random. Failing to react to the non-target doesn't count in this evaluation.
  6. Add one action after stopping it. Once the reaction is stable, practice through the next move. If your reaction rate drops, remove the addition and go back a step.
Checking in the replay after a match

Winning or losing doesn't tell you whether the waiting drill worked. In the replay, count how many times the target action came, how many you recognized, and how many times the input followed.

The target action never came
That isn't a failed reaction. Note that the opponent used other options, and decide whether to watch the same target next match.
You didn't see it
Check whether your targets multiplied along the way and where on the screen you were looking. Practice recognition alone before adding answers.
You saw it but couldn't input
Recognition succeeded. Check the input history and drill the chosen answer on its own.
You stopped it but nothing followed
Grade the reaction and the operation after it separately. As in Iori's video, go back to training mode and check what your character can get.
Practice log template

Fill one per match or per training-mode setup. Count the numbers from your own replays and practice; don't set targets in advance.

  • Opponent's character
  • The action I watch for this time
  • The action I give up this time
  • The answer when I see it
  • Times the target action came
  • Times I recognized it
  • Times the input succeeded
  • Reason for failure: recognition / input / distance / timing
  • Watch the same target next time?
  • The next action to add
Applying it to other matchups

The steps are the same for any character. Choose one action that troubles you, decide on one answer, and accept the rest for now. Once you can react to one, add the next.

The key is to confirm in replays, not by "I feel used to it", when to add a target. Separating the times you recognized the target from the times the input succeeded tells you whether you need practice in dividing attention or in execution.

How to find your own punishes

Starting point: three scenes from Iori's demonstration (video in Japanese). You can follow the practice from the text alone.

  • 5:19 to 5:51: after blocking a Shoryuken. Iori says to practice a punish that starts from a heavy attack or similar, not to settle for a light starter. The point is to decide on the punish beforehand so you don't waste the chance when you block.
  • 7:02 to 7:42: punishing cr.HK. The demonstration is Ken against Ryu. If the punish you want is hard to do on the spot, have another one ready and don't let a blocked move go unpunished. Don't take the video's Ken example as an answer for other characters.
  • 7:44 to 8:48: High Blade Kick and distance. In the demonstration by strength, a block near the tip leaves the opponent far away and the punish no longer reaches. What to take from it is to look at where you blocked the move too, not the old numbers.
The two things to check first for a punish
Frames: does your attack make it in the time you can move?

First check the opponent's move "on block" and your candidate's "startup". A minus on block is the disadvantage of whoever used the move. But don't decide a punish from the table alone: a late input or a different contact timing changes the conditions you assumed.

Distance: does the hitbox reach the opponent?

A fast move isn't a punish if it doesn't reach. The High Blade Kick scene in the video is the way into this check. Don't memorize only "this button against this move": keep with it where you blocked the move when it works.

Reproducing the opponent's move in training mode

Start with a single move. To study the same pairing as the video, set the opponent to Ryu and yourself to Ken, with the control type you normally use; you don't need to assume the video's control type. Note which one you chose.

  1. Fix the conditions. Midscreen or corner, the strength of the opponent's move, gauges and special states. Start with a normal block; don't mix in parry, Burnout or other conditions.
  2. Turn on the input history and the frame display. To compare what you entered and when you moved after blocking.
  3. Control the opponent and record. Do the move once and hold block from during the move until it ends and the dummy is able to block. To study a grounded strike as the punish, use the opponent's crouching guard at first.
  4. Play back only that recording. Block the move and use your candidate. Mix several recordings only after checking the basic condition.

You can't judge anything if the recorded opponent just stands there after the move. Check once that the opponent blocks a grounded strike you deliberately press late. And don't produce the result with the setting that always forces Punish Counter: check with normal hit detection.

Comparing the same move up close and at the tip

First block the move at a close position and try your candidate. Then start further away and try the same candidate where the opponent's move connects near its tip. Don't count the times the opponent's move whiffed in the "punish after block" comparison.

Between close and tip, not only the distance after the block can change but also which moment of the move you blocked. So don't assume a failure at the tip is "lack of reach" from the start: check the frame advantage at each position too. Leave a mark on the floor or a screenshot so you can return to the same position.

Separating "it makes it in time but doesn't reach"
The move didn't come out
Look at the input history first. Check whether you entered it during blockstun or entered something other than the move you wanted, and get back to producing the same move before comparing distances.
The move came out but whiffed
Go back to the closer starting position and compare. Note separately whether it touches up close and whiffs at the tip. Keep the opponent's stance and what the recording does afterward the same.
The move reached but was blocked
The attack arrived when the opponent could already block. Review a late input, your candidate's startup and the actual advantage on block. Also check whether the table's standard value matches the contact conditions of that attempt.
It hit, but you can't tell whether it's a real punish
Check again that the recording blocks after the move. A hit alone doesn't make it guaranteed: check the frame display and the Punish Counter display and reproduce it under the same conditions. Also confirm that no setting is forcing the display.
Comparing punish candidates

At the same position, change the candidate and compare startup and reach separately. From your own character, choose "a fast candidate", "a candidate that looks like it reaches" and "a candidate that's easy to continue from". Don't choose on the idea that a long move always makes it in time or that a fast move always reaches.

The Shoryuken part of the video is about preparing a punish for a big chance. The sweep part also touches on the punish you can use on the spot. Keeping the punish you aim for in practice apart from the one you use first in matches so you don't miss the chance lets you work on both without mixing them up.

Recording successes and failures

One hit doesn't tell you it works at another distance. Copy the format below into your notes and record close and tip separately. It's a form to fill in, not measured results.

  • Date and game version
  • My character and control type
  • Opponent's move, strength and state
  • Midscreen or corner, and the mark for the starting position
  • Where I blocked it, and the advantage I checked
  • Punish candidate and its startup
  • Result: didn't come out / whiff / blocked / hit
  • What I checked in the input, frame and hit displays
  • Result of reproducing it under the same conditions, and the next condition to change
Keep only the punishes you can use in a match

When you finish, choose one punish to go for in your next matches. Not just "press it when I block that move": make it a set with the condition "I confirmed it reaches at this position".

If it fails in a match, before switching to a harder punish, ask whether the situation was the same as the one you recorded. Take the differences (another strength, at the tip, a late input) back to training mode. Don't extend what you tested to conditions you haven't.

Using CAPCOM's official Frame Data

The official data gives the on-block value of the opponent's move and the startup of your candidate. Don't mix up the rows for L, M, H and OD or for special states. The table is material for narrowing down candidates: it doesn't guarantee reach or your input.

Today's goal isn't to memorize many punishes: it's to be able to explain the conditions under which one punish works.

Comparing what your combo enders leave you (Ken)

Material: Iori's Ken setplay video (in Japanese). It demonstrates the idea of arriving at the same offense from several combo starters, and how knockdowns that look the same call for different next actions depending on distance. The five scenes below are enough to follow the comparison steps.

1. Look at the situation left after the combo before maximum damage

Video 0:16 to 1:51

What the video shows

Iori explains choosing an ender not by damage alone but together with how reliable the input is and which offense is available afterward. The point of comparison is whether you keep not only throw and strike but also the option that uses a walk back.

Turning it into your own practice

Damage isn't the first thing to compare. From the same starter, change only the ender and note which actions reach after the knockdown and whether you can finish the input reliably.

2. Change your next movement depending on whether you reached the corner

Video 1:53 to 3:41

What the video shows

Iori demonstrates different approaches after the combo for when it carried to the corner and for when it stayed at midscreen. With the same ender, the next preparation differs with where the opponent and the wall are.

Turning it into your own practice

Don't note only the combo's name: keep "midscreen or corner" and "the movement I made after the ender" together. Check that you aren't failing by repeating the corner steps at midscreen.

3. Test the same offense against both a throw and a strike

Video 3:44 to 7:58

What the video shows

After showing how several starters lead to the same offense, the video records a throw and a strike on the opponent and checks whether the choices that include the walk back work. Scenes where Drive Impact connects differently lead into the same checking procedure.

Turning it into your own practice

One time that it lined up doesn't make it finished. Split the opponent's action after waking up into block, throw and strike, and note what your same movement beats and loses to.

4. Review the position for midscreen, corner and the kind of recovery

Video 10:21 to 12:58

What the video shows

When the opponent back rises at midscreen, you can't reproduce the corner offense as it is. Iori separates the idea of adding a movement from the idea of spending gauge to carry to the corner and change the situation.

Turning it into your own practice

Don't lump it into "this ender lets me attack": check the opponent's recovery and the final position as a set. Switch between normal recovery and back rise, and check again whether your strike and your throw reach after the same movement.

5. With the same numbers, the throw doesn't reach from the tip

Video 13:00 to 13:40

What the video shows

Near the end, the video shows an ender that counts as the same knockdown situation but connected near its tip, so the throw afterward doesn't reach. He switches to another action that closes the distance.

Turning it into your own practice

The timing being right and the next move reaching are separate problems. Look not only at whether the strike is meaty but at whether the throw won't whiff from that position, and split the reason for a failure into "timing" and "distance".

Exercise: comparing ender, position and recovery

If you mix several combos from the start, you can't tell which difference changed the result. Fix one starter and the route up to the ender, and change one item at a time in this order.

  1. Choose one reference combo. Use one you can finish reliably.
  2. Change only the ender. Keep the starter, the inputs in the middle and the starting position the same. Compare the distance after the combo and where the opponent went down.
  3. Change the opponent's recovery. Separate normal recovery and back rise. Don't assume both give the same position: look at where the opponent gets up.
  4. Try your movements one at a time. No movement, a forward dash, a short walk, each on its own. Don't put several movements in at once.
  5. Check the next strike and the throw separately. A meaty strike doesn't mean the throw reaches. Record block, throw and strike on the opponent separately and keep the results apart.
  6. Keep the conditions that failed. Besides the successes, note the failures: the throw whiffed from the tip, the back rise put them out of reach, the input was late.
Separating the reasons it "doesn't line up"
The input isn't consistent
Check in the input history whether the ender or the movement differs each time. Before comparing positions, be able to reproduce the same end state.
The timing is off
The move reaches but loses to the opponent's strike, or is blocked. Watching the frame display, compare the timings with a movement or a whiff in between.
The distance is short
The attack or the throw whiffs. With the same timing, the distance changes when the ender connected at the tip and after a back rise. Go back to the starting position and compare again.
The recovery changed the position
It reaches after a normal recovery and not after a back rise. Don't force the same action on both: decide whether another movement is needed or whether the offense ends there.
The opponent's choice was different
Check which of block, throw or strike you recorded on the opponent. Don't read one success as "setplay that beats everything".
Comparison log template

Treat the items below as one set and fill it in each time you change one ender or recovery. It's a form to fill in, not a list of verified setplay.

  • Date and game version
  • Combo used and starter
  • Ender
  • Midscreen or corner
  • Opponent's recovery
  • My movement
  • The strike or throw I went for next
  • Result: hit / blocked / whiff / lost
  • Reason for failure: input / timing / distance / recovery
  • The one condition to change next
Decide on one form to use in matches

When the comparison is done, don't try to learn every ender: keep one form, "from this starter, at this position, this ender into this action". If it fails in a match, check in the replay whether the conditions were the same, and take only that scene back to training mode.

Reading a coaching session scene by scene (Ryu)

Material: Iori's replay coaching of a Ryu player (video in Japanese). Iori pauses the replay to look at how the player moves at mid range, what they choose after gaining advantage, and how they spend gauge.

1. Moving at mid range without having decided what to wait for

Video 0:48 to 1:19

What was happening
Ryu moves back and forth near the opponent, but for long stretches he neither whiff punishes nor goes in to land f.HP (Solar Plexus Strike).
The problem
Keeping the distance has become the goal itself, and nothing is decided about which move answers what. Just waiting doesn't stop the opponent's actions, and Ryu's offense never starts.
Iori's point
If you aren't going for a whiff punish, don't hover on the spot: use f.HP to go and make contact.
Why it's a problem
At the same mid range, "waiting for a whiff" and "placing or landing a move" mean looking at different places and pressing different buttons. With the purpose vague, you're late to the opponent's movement every time.
How to fix it
The moment you enter mid range, choose one: wait to whiff punish this time, or go touch with f.HP. If it doesn't work, switch to the other choice on the next approach.
Practice to try
Pause your replay and say out loud "what was I waiting for at this moment?". Find three scenes where you can't answer, and in your next matches practice using f.HP in just one of them.
2. Choosing a "counter-hit strike" every time after gaining advantage

Video 1:29 to 2:10

What was happening
Even in situations where Ryu can keep attacking, after f.HP for example, the player lays down strikes on the assumption that the opponent will press a button at once. The video confirms the same choice again and again.
The problem
Choosing a strike without evidence that the opponent presses buttons puts no throw pressure on an opponent who keeps blocking. "Gaining advantage" isn't leading into the guessing game after it.
Iori's point
If the opponent understands the plus situation and is blocking, choose the throw; don't keep counter-hitting with jabs.
Why it's a problem
Against strikes only, the opponent limits the damage by continuing to block. It's because you show the throw that they choose a throw tech or a walk back, and then the shimmy and the strike work.
How to fix it
Right after gaining advantage, check "how did they defend last time?". If they kept blocking, throw; if you saw a tech, shimmy; make the counter-hit strike a candidate only when you saw them press.
Practice to try
Count your next 10 plus situations and note whether you chose strike, throw or shimmy. If it leans to strikes only, show a throw once in the first plus situation of the next match.
3. Sort out normals and the move after a throw before spending gauge

Video 2:43 to 3:51

What was happening
When going in to make contact, the player leans on Drive Rush; and after a forward throw, tries to meaty a strike from far away and the offense breaks off.
The problem
Spending gauge and putting the opponent in a guess have become the same thing. And by ignoring the distance after the throw, the strike doesn't reach and the turn is given away.
Iori's point
First touch with f.HP and spend gauge where it's needed; and after a forward throw at midscreen, walk a little and place f.HP.
Why it's a problem
If you can make contact without gauge, the resource is left for the real offense after it. After a throw, too, you need a move that fits the longer distance, not a strike that doesn't reach.
How to fix it
Before pressing Drive Rush, check once: "can't a normal reach from here?". After a forward throw, don't press the fastest strike automatically: look at the distance and choose between walking a little, placing f.HP and doing nothing.
Practice to try
In training mode, record the situation after a forward throw at midscreen and check the distance for walking a little and using f.HP. In your next matches, for one match, count how many times you made contact with f.HP without Drive Rush.
4. Thinking "whiffing is bad" too much, and never showing the opponent a move

Video 5:18 to 6:41

What was happening
The player keeps going for whiff punishes but almost never uses f.HP and misses chances to make contact. Iori demonstrates whiffing a move from a safe position.
The problem
If you avoid every whiff, the opponent walks forward without respecting Ryu's moves. While you go only for whiff punishes, you hand over the range you could be using.
Iori's point
Separate "a whiff that doesn't need to hit" from "going in to land f.HP where it reaches", and make using f.HP the assignment.
Why it's a problem
Whiffs include not only the mistake of swinging a big move while exposed but also placing a hitbox at a safe distance to stop the opponent's advance. Ban all of them and the ground game itself loses options.
How to fix it
Don't swing vaguely from where your move doesn't reach: choose "the position where it touches if they walk forward". Also see whether their punish reached after your whiff, and separate dangerous distances from safe ones.
Practice to try
In training mode, have the opponent walk back and forth and compare the position where f.HP reaches with the position where their whiff punish reaches after your whiff. In matches, make the goal trying f.HP three times, not the result.
5. Don't keep guarding against button presses that aren't there: compare the mixup with the risk

Video 7:07 to 12:51

What was happening
In situations where Ryu can attack, such as after a blocked jump-in, the player keeps choosing strikes. The opponent isn't actually pressing, yet the throw and the shimmy almost never appear.
The problem
Choosing a strike every time just because "they might press a button" takes no damage from an opponent who defends. It isn't a guess: it's getting ahead of button presses you haven't confirmed.
Iori's point
Use more throws and shimmies and create the places where the opponent presses. Later he shows that sequences with a deliberate gap are also chosen by comparing the opponent's light punish with your own big reward.
Why it's a problem
Opening someone up isn't lining up safe actions only. You need to create a difference between choices: if they keep blocking, the throw and gauge damage remain; if they press, you go for a big punish.
How to fix it
First count the times the opponent really pressed. If they aren't pressing, show the throw, and when a tech appears, move to the shimmy. Use sequences with a gap only when you can explain the reason, comparing the punish you take on failure with the reward on success.
Practice to try
In your replay, count separately the times you laid a strike after gaining advantage and the times the opponent really pressed. If the gap is large, show two throws in the next match and then check the reaction.
Reviewing your own replays

You don't need to fix everything at once. Watch one match first and make a single item your assignment for the next match: count the facts in the replay, narrow the cause down to one, try one thing in the next match, and watch the replay again.
