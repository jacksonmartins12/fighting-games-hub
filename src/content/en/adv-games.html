Applied Game Theory

UniversalThe ideas work in any fighting game. The examples use Street Fighter 6 situations; damage taken from this hub's frame data is named as such, and every other number is an example value for you to replace with your own measurements.

Decisions by the Numbers looked at one option at a time: how often it has to work to be worth it. That's enough when the opponent always does the same thing. It isn't when they're also guessing, and guessing against you. Game theory is the tool for that kind of situation, and it answers questions players usually settle by feel: when you should always do the same thing, how often to use each option when you shouldn't, what a knockdown is really worth, and how hard you can lean on a habit before it costs you.

You don't need the math to use this chapter. The solver below does the arithmetic, and the text explains what the results mean. If you want to see where the numbers come from, open the "For the math" boxes.

Write the Situation as a Table

Pick a moment that keeps coming back: your opponent wakes up next to you, you're plus after a blocked string, a round starts. Write your options as rows and theirs as columns. In each cell, put what you gain when that option meets that answer, in damage: positive when you come out ahead, negative when they do. Here's the simplest oki there is, with the numbers used in the rest of the chapter:

You ↓ · They →BlockTech
Meaty strike01,800
Throw1,2000

A blocked meaty gains nothing here. A meaty that catches a tech attempt is a Counter Hit, worth an example 1,800 with the combo. A throw against someone blocking does 1,200 (Ryu's throws in this hub's table). A teched throw does nothing.

The table assumes two things. Both players choose at the same time: neither sees the other's choice in time to change their own. And both have a rough idea of what each outcome is worth. When an option can be seen and answered on reaction, the first assumption breaks; When They Can React shows what to do then.

For the math: notation
  • The table is the payoff matrix A; aij is what you (the rows) gain when you pick option i and they pick answer j. What you gain, they lose, so it's a zero-sum game.
  • A mixed strategy is a list of frequencies that adds up to 1: yours is p = (p1, …, pm), theirs q = (q1, …, qn). Always doing the same thing is a list with a single 1.
  • When both sides mix, your average gain is pᵀAq = Σi Σj pi aij qj. (Aq)i is what option i earns against their mix; (pᵀA)j is what answer j gives up against yours.

When You Shouldn't Mix

Sometimes the table has an answer that's best no matter what the other side does. Then there's nothing to guess: that side should do it every time, and the other side can't fix it by mixing. Game theory calls that cell a saddle point.

Example: oki with no answer to the reversal

You have a meaty strike, a throw and a shimmy. The opponent can block, tech, or use OD Shoryuken, which Ryu's table in this hub lists at 1,600 damage and fully invincible on frames 1–8. As the hub's table of defensive options says, an invincible reversal beats strikes, throws and shimmies alike: on wakeup, the shimmy is still walking forward when it comes out. So the reversal costs you 1,600 whatever you pick. The defender should reversal every time, and no mix of your three options changes that. Load "Oki with no answer to the reversal" in the solver to see it.

The fix isn't a better mix of the same options. It's a new option that beats the reversal: blocking and waiting (the bait). A blocked OD Shoryuken is −40 and gets punished. The four-option example below adds it.

For the math: maximin and minimax

For each of your options, its worst cell (the row minimum) is what it guarantees. The best of those guarantees is your maximin. For the defender, each column's maximum is the most that answer can cost them, and the smallest of those is their minimax:

maximin = maxi minj aij ≤ minimax = minj maxi aij

When the two are equal, the cell where they meet is the smallest in its row and the largest in its column: a saddle point. In the example, every row's worst cell is −1,600 (the reversal), and the reversal's column never goes above −1,600, so both sides of the inequality are −1,600.

Options You Can Cross Out

If one of your options does worse than another against every single answer, never use it: it's dominated. Cross it out. Sometimes crossing one out makes another one useless too, so check again. The same goes for the defender: an answer that costs them more than another answer against everything you do will never be part of their best play.

Be careful with options that are only "never better and sometimes worse" (weakly dominated). Crossing those out never hurts you, but it can hide another way to play that's just as good. Treat them as a hint, not a rule. The solver flags both kinds.

Why Mixing Works (and How Much)

When there's no saddle point, anything you always do can be read and punished. The answer is to mix, in the right proportions. The right mix has one property: it makes every answer the opponent uses equally good for them. Then reading you is worthless. Whatever they pick, you get the same average, and that average is the value of the situation: the most you can guarantee against a perfect opponent, and the least they can hold you to. The defender's right mix works the same way from their side.

This leads to the most surprising rule in the chapter: how often you should use each option depends on the opponent's payoffs, not yours. Your mix is whatever makes their answers equally good, so if your throw starts doing more damage, it's the defender who has to change (tech more), and your own throw frequency can go down. The throw loop below shows exactly that.

Example: strike or throw

In the table at the top of the chapter, the solver says: strike 40% of the time and throw 60%; the defender should block 60% and tech 40%; and the situation is worth 720 per knockdown.

Check it against the defender's mix. Your strike earns 1,800 whenever they tech, 40% of the time: 720 on average. Your throw earns 1,200 whenever they block, 60% of the time: also 720. Both of your options earn the same, so no read of yours beats their mix. The same check from their side: against your 40/60, blocking gives up 60% × 1,200 = 720, and teching gives up 40% × 1,800 = 720.

For the math: the minimax theorem, the 2 × 2 formula and larger tables

John von Neumann proved in 1928 that every finite zero-sum game has a value v and optimal mixes p* and q* such that:

maxp minq pᵀAq = minq maxp pᵀAq = v

With p* you earn at least v whatever they do; with q* they give up at most v whatever you do. In zero-sum games these minimax strategies are the same thing as Nash equilibria. At the equilibrium, every option you use earns exactly v against q*, and every option you leave out earns at most v (the indifference principle); the same holds for the defender.

The 2 × 2 formula. For rows (a, b) and (c, d) with no saddle point, let D = a − b − c + d. Your frequency of row 1 is p = (d − c) / D, theirs of column 1 is q = (d − b) / D, and the value is v = (ad − bc) / D. In the strike-or-throw table, a = 0, b = 1,800, c = 1,200, d = 0 and D = −3,000: p = 0.4, q = 0.6, v = 720.

Larger tables. Add a constant k to every cell so all entries are at least 1, then solve the linear program

maximize Σj yj subject to Σj (aij + k) yj ≤ 1 for every row i, y ≥ 0

The defender's mix is q* = y / Σy, the value is v = 1 / Σy − k, and your mix p* comes from the dual of the same program. The solver uses the simplex method with Bland's rule, which can't cycle. An equilibrium always exists but doesn't have to be unique; when another mix is just as good, the solver says so.

Worked Example: Oki With Four Options

Load "Oki: 4 options against 4 answers". Your options are a meaty strike, a throw, a shimmy, and blocking to bait the reversal; theirs are block, tech, a fast button and OD Shoryuken. Who beats whom follows the hub's table of defensive options: the fast button beats throws and loses to meaties and shimmies; the reversal beats everything but the bait. Teching is pressing throw, so against an attacker who stands and blocks, the defender's throw comes out and grabs them. The example is a Ryu mirror, and the damage that comes from this hub's tables is Ryu's: 1,200 for either throw and 1,600 for OD Shoryuken. The punish on a blocked OD Shoryuken uses the same rough 3,000 as Defense Is a Bet Too. The rest (1,800 for a Counter Hit meaty combo, 2,500 for a Punish Counter after a shimmy, 1,500 for whiff-punishing a button, 1,000 for a button that beats your throw) are example values.

You ↓ · They →BlockTechFast buttonOD Shoryuken
Meaty strike01,8001,800−1,600
Throw1,2000−1,000−1,600
Shimmy02,5001,500−1,600
Block and bait0−1,20003,000

The solution: the situation is worth about 295 per knockdown. Your best mix is about 9% meaty, 25% throw, 25% shimmy and 41% bait; the defender's is about 59% block, 8% tech, 20% fast button and 13% reversal. What that tells you:

  • Every option on both sides gets used. Each option beats something the others don't, so none can be crossed out and none should be used every time. Every option you use earns exactly 295 against their mix, and every answer they use gives up exactly 295 against yours.
  • The bait is your most frequent option. It's the only one that beats the reversal, and the reversal is what keeps the defender afloat. Used only about one time in eight, the reversal still makes you wait about 41% of the time.
  • The meaty is your rarest option. The shimmy covers the same two answers (tech and fast button) and pays more against the tech; the meaty only stays in the mix because it pays more against the button.
  • The result depends on your numbers. If a blocked meaty is worth 400 to you because you keep the turn, the meaty jumps to about 46% and the shimmy drops out, with the value rising to about 328. If the punish after the bait is only 2,000, you bait about half the time, the meaty drops out and the value falls to about 208. Before trusting a mix, change each number you're unsure of and see whether the answer changes.

Damage Isn't the Whole Story: What Happens Next

A cell rarely ends the exchange. A blocked meaty keeps you on offense; a throw gives another knockdown and another oki; a teched throw resets to something close to neutral. What a cell is really worth is its damage plus what the next situation is worth. When a result leads straight back to the same situation, like a throw that sets up the same oki again (a throw loop), tick ↻ on that cell and the solver adds that in for you.

Example: a throw loop

Take the strike-or-throw table and suppose your throw puts them right back in the same oki (load "Strike or throw, with a throw loop"). Each successful throw is now worth its 1,200 plus another round of the same situation.

The situation goes from 720 to about 987 per knockdown. And the mixes move the way the rule above predicts, not the way intuition does: the throw is now worth about 2,187 against a blocking player, so the defender has to tech more (55% instead of 40%), and you throw less (45% instead of 60%). Your mix changed because their payoffs changed.

A loop only has a finite value if the defender has a way out that they actually use. If a cell loops and nothing they can do ever ends it, the solver says so instead of giving a number. In a real match the round ends first; Play for the Round covers what that changes.

For the math: continuation values and loops

The honest payoff of a cell is aij = damageij + V(next situationij). Situations linked this way form a stochastic game (Lloyd Shapley, 1953), which also has values, found by value iteration: start every V at 0, solve each table with the current values in its cells, replace the values with the results and repeat until they stop changing. For a situation that loops back to itself, the value is a fixed point:

V = val( A + R·V ), where Rij = 1 for cells that loop back

In the throw loop, the throw cell is 1,200 + V, and the 2 × 2 formula gives:

V = 1,800 (1,200 + V) / (3,000 + V) ⇒ V² + 1,200 V − 2,160,000 = 0 ⇒ V ≈ 987

The throw cell is then 1,200 + 987 = 2,187.

Balanced Mix or Exploiting Habits

The balanced mix is a guarantee, not the most you can earn. Against a real opponent, who has habits, you can earn more by leaning toward the option that beats what they actually do (the best response). The price is exposure: the more you lean, the more you lose if they notice and adapt.

Example: the ten wakeups

Reading Habits as Percentages counted ten wakeups: 5 blocks, 3 techs, one button and one reversal. In the four-option oki, using the estimate from the next section (43%, 29%, 14%, 14%), each option earns: meaty about 543, throw 143, shimmy 700, bait 86 (this player techs often, and every tech throws you while you wait). The best response is the shimmy, because this player techs far more than a balanced defender would (about 29% against 8%).

0% (the balanced mix)295295

100% (only shimmy)700−1,600

Look at the first row: the balanced mix earns exactly 295 against this player, the same as against anyone. When the defender's best mix uses every answer, the balanced mix earns the same whatever they do. It protects you, but it never profits from a habit; profit only comes from leaning away from it. In this example, every 100 points of extra profit against the habit cost about 470 if they adapt.

Two rules for exploiting with discipline:

  • Lean as far as your evidence goes. Ten wakeups is very little (next section). Move part of the mix, not all of it, and move more as the count grows.
  • Only risk what you've already won. While the habit is paying you more than the situation is worth, you can afford to lean further, as long as what you risk never exceeds that extra. When it stops paying, go back to the balanced mix. This is the idea behind "safe exploitation" (see the sources at the end).

How Much to Trust a Few Rounds

Counts from a few rounds are noisy. An answer you saw 0 times in 6 isn't an answer they never use. Two simple habits keep you honest, and the solver applies both:

  • Add one to every count before turning it into a percentage. With 5 blocks, 3 techs, one button and one reversal in 10 wakeups, count 6, 4, 2 and 2 out of 14: about 43%, 29%, 14% and 14%. Small counts get pulled toward the middle, and nothing ever shows 0% or 100%.
  • Remember how wide the range is. With 10 observations, 5 blocks means anywhere between about 24% and 76%; one reversal means between about 2% and 40%. To halve that range you need four times as many observations: 20 blocks in 40 is the same 50%, but the range shrinks to about 35%–65%.

Habits also change during a set, often right after they cost the player something. Count by game, not by set, and trust the latest game more than the first.

For the math: Laplace's rule and the Wilson interval

Adding one to each count is Laplace's rule of succession, the mean of a Dirichlet posterior with one imaginary observation per answer: with nj observations of answer j out of N, across k answers, q̂j = (nj + 1) / (N + k). The ranges are 95% Wilson score intervals (E. B. Wilson, 1927) for each raw share. Their width shrinks with the square root of the sample size, which is why four times the data halves the range.

Play for the Round, Not the Damage

Everything above is about average damage. What you actually want is to win the round, and near the end of a round those aren't the same thing.

  • Lethal changes the table. If the opponent has 1,100 health left, a 1,200 throw and a 1,800 combo do the same thing: they win the round. Write "round won" in both cells, not their damage, and solve again.
  • Behind, take risks; ahead, avoid them. When you need more damage than any safe sequence gives, a risky option with a lower average can be the right play, because it's the only one that can get there. When you're ahead, prefer the option whose worst case still keeps you ahead.
  • The solver still works. Your round win is their round loss, so only the unit changes. Put your estimated chance of winning the round in each cell (for example 100 for "round won", 0 for "round lost", something in between otherwise), and the mixes it gives you are the ones that give you the best chance of winning the round.

When They Can React

The table assumes nobody sees the other's choice in time. That's true of a meaty against a wakeup option, which play out in the same few frames, but not of everything. Some options can be seen and answered: the Drive System chapter recommends answering a Drive Impact with your own, on reaction. Against an option that always gets answered on reaction, there's no guess at all: it's worth whatever the reaction leaves it.

Real reactions work only part of the time. If you react correctly to an option, say, 70% of the time, its cell is worth 70% of the result against your reaction plus 30% of the result against whatever you were guessing. Measure your rate in training mode, with the dummy doing the option at random, and put that blended number in the table. An option that only works when the opponent fails to react should be used less as they get better at reacting, and the solver shows how much less.

For the math: reaction as a probability

With a reaction success rate r, the cell becomes a weighted average:

aij = r · (payoff against the correct reaction) + (1 − r) · (payoff against your guess j)

An option answered on reaction every time (r = 1) turns that part of the game into a sequential one, solved backward: the reacting side picks the best answer to what it saw.

Where the Model Stops

  • People aren't random. A balanced mix only protects you if your choices are unpredictable in order, not just in proportion. Alternating meaty, throw, meaty, throw has the right percentages and is completely readable.
  • The game repeats, and players learn. Across a set, the opponent updates their picture of your mix, and you of theirs. The balanced mix is the right default when you know nothing, and the safe place to return to when your reads stop working; it isn't the most you can earn.
  • The numbers are estimates. Damage depends on spacing, screen position and your execution; frequencies come from small samples. Check how much the mix moves when you change an uncertain number before you trust its decimals.
  • Few situations stand alone. Drive Gauge, Super meter, the corner and the timer change what every later situation is worth. Linking situations covers some of that; the rest is judgment.
