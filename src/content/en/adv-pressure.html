Pressure and Defense in Frames

UniversalThe gap rule works in any game. The checker, and the sections marked SF6, use Street Fighter 6 frame data and systems.

Mechanics teaches the gap rule with one example. This chapter applies it to every pair of moves of your character at once, shows how Drive Rush and Burnout change the numbers, and then looks at the same situations from the defender's side.

The Gap, Revisited

After a blocked move that leaves you +A, a next move with S frames of startup leaves a gap of S − A frames. If your opponent's fastest normal has F frames of startup (4 for almost everyone in SF6):

GapWhat happens
0 or lessTrue block string: they're still blocking when the next hit arrives.
1 to F − 1Frame trap: any normal they press gets hit, usually as a Counter Hit.
Exactly FTrades with their fastest normal.
More than FA hole: their fastest normal hits you first. Throws (5 frames) win from a gap of 6; at exactly 5 the throw and your strike start on the same frame, and in SF6 the strike wins.

Invincible reversals, Drive Parry and Drive Reversal ignore these numbers: they can beat a frame trap and, in Drive Reversal's case, even a true block string.

The "Oki and pressure, calculated" section of every character guide applies the same rule to all of the fighter's plus moves at once, with and without Drive Rush, and lists the true block strings and the frame traps against a 4-frame normal that each one leads to.

Drive Rush and Burnout Change the MathSF6

The first normal out of a Drive Rush gets +4 frames on hit and on block. An example from Ryu's data: Stand MP is −1 on block, and out of Drive Rush it's +3. From +3, Stand LP (4 frames) leaves a gap of 1 and Crouch MP (6 frames) a gap of 3: both are frame traps against a 4-frame jab, and Crouch MP is the better button to turn a Counter Hit into a combo. Without the Drive Rush, the same Crouch MP after Stand MP leaves a gap of 7.

Burnout works the same way for the attacker. A player in Burnout spends 4 extra frames in blockstun after every blocked hit (see Burnout), so every gap shrinks by 4: a frame trap with a gap of 3 becomes a true block string, and a move that was −2 becomes +2. The two boxes in the checker add those 4 frames.

Drive Rush isn't free: 3 bars as a cancel, 1 from a parry (see Drive Rush). Spending 3 bars to turn −1 into +3 is worth it when it leads to damage or to the corner, not as a habit.

Finding and Proving Your Own Frame Traps

  1. Start from your plus and near-plus moves. In the checker, pick each move you actually use in pressure and note the frame traps it lists. Prefer follow-ups that combo on Counter Hit.
  2. Check reach. The checker knows time, not distance. Do the string against a blocking dummy, standing and crouching, and see if the second hit still connects after pushback.
  3. Prove the gap. Record the dummy pressing its fastest normal and set it to play back right after blocking (the reversal settings). If your string counter-hits it every time, the trap is real. Try the dummy's throw too.
  4. Find what still beats it. Switch the recording to a reversal, a Drive Reversal and a Drive Parry. Knowing what beats your string tells you when to stop pressing and block.

Reading the Opponent's Pressure

The same method works backwards. Open their character in the checker and in the full frame data, and note three things: which moves leave them minus enough to press, which strings are true block strings (don't press, wait) and which have gaps you can mash or jump through. Then watch a replay where they pressured you and check where you pressed and where you should have. That's how you stop guessing on defense.

Delay Tech, in Frames

Delay tech is an option select: you block for the first few frames after the attacker's plus move, then press throw. If they went for a strike, you were already blocking. If they went for a throw, your late tech catches it.

It loses to anything that's happening when you press throw. A frame trap with a small gap hits your throw attempt as a Counter Hit, and a shimmy leaves your throw whiffing in front of them. That's why frame traps and shimmies get stronger the more an opponent delay techs. Run the checker on your opponent's character to find which of their plus moves lead to frame traps: those are the moments when delay tech is most dangerous for you.

Fuzzy Defense: Block First, Act Second

Delay tech belongs to a family. dood.gg calls them fuzzy techniques: you hold back to block, and you time a second action so it only comes out in the frames where the attacker didn't attack. One input keeps you safe against the obvious strike and still answers something else. The attacker's counterpart is the delayed attack: waiting a moment longer before pressing, to catch whatever the defender's fuzzy does.

The attacker's answer: delayed attacks

From the attacker's side, the trick is to wait as if you were blocking their invincible reversal, and only then act. If they reversal, you were blocking. If they fuzzed or pressed their fastest button, your late attack catches it.

None of these is a trick that always works. They're layers of timing, and each one has an answer. Learn them in this order: delay tech first, then one fuzzy button for your character, then the delayed versions so you know what beats you. Practice each one against the dummy with the protocol from Reading and Measuring, recording the attacker's options at different timings.

Option Selects: One Input, Two Outcomes

An option select is an input that does different things depending on what the opponent did. In SF6 most of them come from buffering: you enter a command during another action, and it only comes out if a certain condition happens. dood.gg's input buffering guide lists many; these are the ones most characters can use:

  • Cancel buffer. Enter a special (or Drive Rush, with the parry button) while a cancelable normal comes out. Cancels only happen when the normal connects, so the special comes out on hit or block and never on whiff. Use it with specials that are safe on block.
  • Super Art against Drive Reversal. During the second light attack of a meaty light string, enter your Super Art motion. If they Drive Reversal, the Super comes out and punishes; if they block, nothing happens.
  • After parrying a fireball. Buffer an attack, a jump or a Drive Rush so it only comes out if the parry was a Perfect Parry.
  • Target combo buffer. Enter the special move's motion first, then press the target combo's next button. If you confirm in time, you get the special cancel; if your confirm is late, the target combo still comes out (dood.gg's example is Manon's À Terre).

Two cautions. First, check what else the buffered motion can trigger: a motion that's harmless for one character can leak a special for another (dood.gg's example is A.K.I., whose OD Snake Step can come out of a Super Art buffer). Second, build your own the same way they were found: pick two situations, find an input that does nothing in one and the right thing in the other, and test both in training mode.

Getting Out: When to Spend Resources

Against pressure with no holes, the only exits cost something: a Drive Reversal (2 bars), an OD reversal (2 bars) or a reversal Super Art. Before spending, compare what the pressure is likely to cost you if you keep blocking with what you lose if your escape is baited. The calculator in Decisions by the Numbers gives you the success rate you need.

  • Keep 2 bars for an escape when you're cornered; without them, your only options are blocking and guessing.
  • Don't spend your last bars on a Drive Reversal: Burnout makes every following string tighter by 4 frames.
  • When their frame traps start catching you, stop pressing and block. The next thing they'll go for is a throw, and that's when a tech or a jump wins.

Defense Drill (15 minutes)

  1. Record the dummy close to you with four options in four slots: a meaty strike, a throw, a shimmy (walk forward and back) and a frame trap from the checker.
  2. Set random playback and play the defender: block by default, delay tech, and against the shimmy simply keep blocking.
  3. Count how often each slot beats you. The one that wins most is the habit an opponent will find first.
  4. Repeat with the dummy as your most common opponent's character.
