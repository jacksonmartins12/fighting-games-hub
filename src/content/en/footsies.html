Footsies Deep Dive: The Art of Mid-Range

UniversalThe ideas in this chapter apply to any fighting game. Examples come from SF6, and sections marked SF6 only apply to it. For how other games differ, see Fighting Game Families.

Operational Definition:

"Footsies" is the mid-range game of controlling space with walking, normal moves and pokes, with the goal of landing the first clean hit (often leading to a knockdown) or making the opponent commit to a mistake you can punish.

But this is only the surface definition. Really, it's split-second critical thinking at mid-range: reading what the opponent wants to do and making it fail.

The 35 Elements of Footsies (Structured Summary)

The Street Fighter Footsies Handbook (written by Maj on sonichurricane.com) is organized into ten chapters that describe 35 numbered elements/tactics. Its examples come from older games (Super Turbo, the Alpha series, CvS2, SF4), but the principles carry over to SF6. The groups below follow the handbook's chapters:

  • Elements 1-3 (Footsies 101): Stepping in and out of poke range, baiting reversals with spacing, throwing a hesitant opponent
  • Elements 4-6 (Feints & Baits): Whiff baits, beating overused feints, playing against meter fishing
  • Elements 7-9 (Corner Mastery): Safe corner positioning, faking the tick throw, max-range light pokes
  • Elements 10-12 (Super Meter): Baiting supers, intentional gaps against reversal supers, staying calm after a reset
  • Elements 13-15 (Jumping): Jumping fireballs, crossups off a baited sweep, jump attack ranges and mixups
  • Elements 16-18 (Close Range): Step-forward combos, delayed low attacks, taking over once they stop pressing buttons
  • Elements 19-22 (Minor Tricks): Low-to-standing light bait, earning an extra step, walking back after a blocked jump-in, spacing pokes so the counterpoke whiffs
  • Elements 23-26 (Hopkicks): Safe hopkick setups, hopkicks up close, divekicks as hopkicks, defending against them
  • Elements 27-30 (Jumping in Footsies): Jumping after a knockdown, jumping over a whiffed slow attack, chaos into crossup, stopping crossups
  • Elements 31-35 (Misdirection): Meaningless safe actions, skipping beats, sudden offense, wakeup c.LP, doing weird things

    • The 3 Fundamental Elements (Deep Dive)

    Element 1: Step In & Back Out (The Root)

    You walk into opponent's poke range, then quickly exit WITHOUT attacking.

    Why it works:

    • You test his reactions
    • You learn his timing
    • If he tries to poke, he whiffs, you punish
    • If he doesn't try, you learn he's waiting

    Depth: The handbook illustrates this element with Mike Watson's Hyper Fighting Guile spending two consecutive full rounds toying with his opponent's natural reactions. It works especially well after long stretches of cautious zoning, or with charge characters who rarely walk forward, because the sudden step forward provokes a response.

    Element 2: Leave Them Just Outside Reversal Range (The Bait)

    Learn which of your combos and attack strings leave the opponent barely outside the range of their reversal (DP, invincible Super Art). You look vulnerable, they spend the reversal, it whiffs, and you punish it.

    Whiff Punish Math:

    • A whiffed reversal (DP, invincible Super Art) has long recovery, and in SF6 hitting it during recovery gives you a Punish Counter
    • To punish, your move's startup must hit before that recovery ends, and the move must reach from where you are standing
    • Because the reversal whiffed, you can usually start your most damaging punish combo instead of a light poke

    Risk: If your spacing is off and they're inside reversal range, the "bait" just gets you hit. Learn the exact ranges in training mode. (The handbook's example is the final exchange of the B3 SFA2 tournament between John Choi's Ken and Alex Valle's Sagat.)

    Element 3: Throw Setup via Poke Pattern (The Transition)

    You poke consistently in the same range until the opponent gets hesitant about pressing buttons.

    Then you walk up and throw.

    Psychology: Opponent thinks "if he pokes again and I attack, I'll get swept".

    He doesn't attack. You don't poke. You throw. He takes damage.

    Counter Play: If he learns this, he can:

    • Throw tech or throw you first
    • Jump or back off when he sees you walking in
    • Press a fast button as you walk up (beats a throw attempt that hasn't started)

    Note: This only works after the poke pattern is established. The handbook's example (John Choi's CvS2 Sakura) points out that the same gutsy throws would never have worked at the start of the round.

    Don't Drop Your Best Poke After One Punish

    The handbook's warning: getting swept for whiffing your best poke costs about a tenth of your life. Taking that poke out of your game because you're afraid of the next sweep costs every option the poke gave you: the space it held, the whiff punishes it set up, the throws that came after it. Being outplayed once doesn't mean the opponent has you figured out. Keep the poke in the rotation and change when or where you use it.

    Zoning: The Geometry of Advantage

    Zoning is closely tied to footsies: it means acquiring and maintaining the ranges where your tools are better than your opponent's.

    Example: Guile vs Zangief

    Zone 1 (Long Range): 2/3 of screen. Guile dominates with Sonic Booms. Zangief has to work hard to get in.

    Zone 2 (Mid Range): Guile's c.MK range. Guile advantage. c.MK can be cancelled into Sonic Boom to keep Zangief out.

    Zone 3 (Close Range): Zangief's command-grab range. Zangief dominates. One Screw Piledriver deals heavy damage and gives him a knockdown to start his offense again.

    Strategy:

    • If you're Guile: Maintain distance. Never let Zangief get close.
    • If you're Zangief: Advance patiently, gaining ground a little at a time (in SF6, Drive Impact, Drive Parry and Drive Rush help against fireballs and to close the gap). Your threat grows the closer you get.

    Elements 4-6: Feints & Baiting Tactics

    Element 4: Whiff Baits

    Whiff a quick light attack (the handbook's example: a crouching light kick a few pixels outside their sweep range) so the opponent thinks they can counterattack, then punish the move they throw out.

    Execution:

    • Stand just outside the range of the opponent's favorite counterpoke (e.g. their sweep)
    • Whiff a light attack: it recovers quickly, so it is a cheap feint
    • Opponent sees the whiff, tries to punish it, and their move whiffs short
    • You punish the whiffed counterattack

    Psychology: Opponent is frustrated (they "had" you). Next time, they're more aggressive, making them more predictable.

    Element 5: Beating Overused Feints

    When the opponent keeps using feints (whiffed light attacks, step in/step out) in a predictable rhythm, take a quick step forward and attack with your strongest combo in sync with their rhythm.

    Pattern Recognition:

    • You notice: "He always whiffs c.LK, then backs off, then repeats"
    • The feint itself does nothing; it only works if you react to it
    • You time a step forward to land as their feint recovers or as they step back in
    • Your best combo starter hits them in the middle of their own pattern

    Evolution: As they adapt, they stop feinting so predictably. You've "forced" them to change. The handbook's Rule #1 applies to both of you: never become predictable.

    Element 6: Playing Against Meter Fishing

    When the opponent fishes for super meter at mid-range (throwing out special moves to build gauge), stick to quick normal moves and avoid slow special moves, or bait their super from a safe distance instead.

    Trade-off Calculation:

    • Quick normals let you interrupt their specials or block on time
    • Slow special moves give a meter-holding opponent something to punish with super
    • Once they have the meter, every unsafe move you make is a bigger risk

    SF6 Note: Super Art gauge is separate from the Drive Gauge, and Super Arts come in levels 1-3 (plus a Critical Art at low health). Track both gauges: an opponent sitting on 3 bars or in Critical Art range changes what is safe for you.

    Elements 7-9: Corner Mastery

    Element 7: Corner Positioning

    Whenever you knock someone into the corner, establish a safe position slightly outside their reach. The corner is dangerous for the defender because they can't back away from your attacks.

    Asymmetry:

    • Opponent in corner: They can't walk back to make your pokes whiff, so they have to press buttons, jump, or block. When you sense them stepping forward, poke (e.g. c.MK); if they counterpoke, hit the extended limb or throw them back into the corner.
    • You in corner: Any mistake can lead to heavy damage. You must defend patiently and choose your escape options carefully.

    Strategic Goal: Keep them cornered and make them commit first. Experienced opponents step forward before counterpoking, so you may need slightly more space than you think.

    Element 8: Faking the Tick Throw in the Corner

    A tick throw is a light attack that forces the opponent to block, followed by a throw as soon as they leave blockstun. Shortly after a tick throw has worked, repeat the same sequence but step backward instead of throwing.

    Setup Pattern:

    • Opponent blocks your light attack, expecting the throw again
    • They try to tech (or throw) as soon as blockstun ends
    • You've stepped back, so their throw attempt whiffs
    • You punish the whiffed throw with a damaging combo (in SF6, a whiffed throw has long recovery and you get a Punish Counter; this is the "shimmy")

    Corner Multiplier: In the corner they can't walk out of the situation, so they are forced to take bigger risks, which makes the bait more likely to work.

    Element 9: Max-Range Light Pokes in the Corner

    Following a basic combo or block string, poke with a light attack from its maximum distance.

    The Loop:

    • The corner keeps them from backing away, so your light attack doesn't whiff
    • If they block, you've lost nothing: light attacks are low-risk
    • If they press a button, your faster attack stuffs it
    • After a few of these they hesitate, which is your chance to walk up and throw

    Counter: The cornered player has to find a moment to jump, use a reversal, or take a risk with a button. In SF6, Drive Reversal (2 Drive bars, from blockstun) is one more way out, but it is punishable on block and impossible while in Burnout.

    Elements 10-12: Super Meter Management in Footsies

    Element 10: Baiting Supers

    When the opponent has a super ready, stay far away for a while, then walk into their c.MK range and immediately block low.

    Scenario:

    • Opponent has a super that can be cancelled from c.MK (or used on reaction)
    • You walk into their c.MK range, looking like you're about to poke
    • They poke and cancel into super, but you're already blocking
    • You punish the blocked super, or at least you've burned their meter

    Value: Even without a punish, you've neutralized the threat of their super meter for a while. In SF6, many Level 1-3 Super Arts are unsafe on block, so a blocked super is often a free punish.

    Element 11: Creating Gaps Against Reversal Supers

    Create an intentional gap in your attack string at the point where the opponent usually tries a reversal super, and fill it with the appropriate counter: a move that beats their specific reversal.

    Execution:

    • Find the spot in your pressure where they like to reversal (often a gap between links)
    • Instead of the usual follow-up, use a move that avoids or beats their super
    • The handbook's example: a MK Hurricane Kick doesn't combo from s.LK, but it is invincible to fireballs and therefore to Ryu's fireball super
    • The simplest SF6 version: leave the gap and block (or Drive Parry), then punish the reversal

    Read Depth: If they don't reversal, you continue your offense. If they do, you punish them.

    Element 12: Staying Calm After a Reset

    When an aggressive opponent willingly pushes you away and resets to neutral, don't commit to a big move right away. Test the waters by whiffing a single low jab as counter-bait, or simply block patiently to see what they have in mind.

    Why It Matters:

    • A reset is usually a deliberate choice, and often a setup
    • Rushing in immediately is exactly what they may be waiting for
    • A low-risk action (blocking, a quick whiffed jab) shows you what they intend
    • Once you know, you can respond with the right tool

    Result: Sometimes remaining calm through a tense moment is all it takes to avoid defeat. The handbook's Rule #3 is related: constantly monitor your opponent's state of mind.

    Elements 13-15: Jumping & Anti-Air Interactions

    Element 13: Fireball Jumping

    Give your opponent a good reason to throw a fireball, then jump over it.

    Precision:

    • First establish that you don't need to jump to win: play the ground game until they feel safe throwing fireballs
    • Learn the distance where a jump over their fireball lands you in punish range
    • Jump as the fireball comes out, not after it is already on top of you
    • A fireball's recovery leaves them unable to anti-air, so you get a jump-in combo

    Flowchart: They throw fireball → You jump it → You land and attack → They block or get hit. In SF6, Drive Impact, Drive Parry/Perfect Parry and Drive Rush are additional answers to fireballs.

    Element 14: Crossup off a Baited Sweep

    Set up a crossup by baiting a sweep at close range.

    Setup:

    • Learn when your opponent likes to sweep at close range
    • Make yourself look like a sweep target
    • Jump over the sweep as it comes out
    • They can't anti-air while recovering from the sweep, so your crossup lands

    Risk: This is high-risk: if they don't sweep, you've jumped into a ready opponent. Only use it once you've accurately read their sweep habits.

    Element 15: Jump Attack Ranges & Mixups

    Analyze your jump attack ranges and use them to build a mixup.

    Key Questions:

    • From which distances does each jump attack hit in front, cross up, or whiff?
    • Which jumps land you in throw range, and which land just outside?
    • What will you do on landing: combo, throw, low, or empty jump?

    Rule of thumb: Before you leave the ground, know what you intend to do on landing. If you don't have a clear answer, you're jumping into an uppercut.

    Elements 16-18: Close Range

    Element 16: Step Forward Combos

    If your character has a damaging combo starting with c.LK, land it by taking a step forward after forcing the opponent to block (a light attack, a jump attack, or a knockdown).

    Execution:

    • Opponent blocks something at close range
    • You take a step forward: it looks like a throw attempt
    • They react (try to tech, mash a button, or just keep blocking)
    • You hit c.LK into your combo, timed to their habit

    Adjust the timing: Against button-mashers, time the c.LK to stuff their jab. Against patient players, you can walk forward for quite a while before attacking.

    Element 17: Delayed Low Attacks

    A more advanced variation of Element 16 with a slower low attack such as c.MK, timed to hit the opponent as they recover and try to act.

    The handbook's example:

    • Daigo's ST Ryu knocks down Jason Cole's Dhalsim and crosses him up
    • After the blocked crossup, Ryu walks backward for a long time
    • Then he times c.MK to hit Dhalsim low just as Dhalsim stands up to counter-throw

    Note: This is useful for characters without strong c.LK combos. Since you're not confirming a combo, end with a move that leaves you safe if blocked.

    Element 18: Uppercut That!

    Elements 16-17 teach the opponent that pressing buttons at close range is dangerous. Once they stop pressing buttons, you get to walk forward at will.

    What changes:

    • The opponent becomes passive at close range
    • You can walk in, throw, and pressure with less risk
    • When they finally try to break out with a desperate button or jump, you're ready to punish or anti-air it (with a DP if you have one)

    Rule #4 (handbook): Tactics are more reliable than gimmicks. Build your gameplan around solid tactics such as real 50/50 mixups, not one-time tricks the opponent will eventually figure out.

    Elements 19-22: Minor Tricks & Positioning

    Element 19: Low Light Bait into Standing Light

    Whiff a crouching light attack as bait, then cancel its recovery into a standing light attack that avoids or beats their response.

    How it works:

    • The crouching light attack looks like a careless whiff
    • The opponent tries to punish it with a poke
    • Because light attacks can be chained or cancelled quickly, you're already into the standing light attack
    • The standing attack catches their poke instead

    Value: Turns a "free whiff punish" into a trap. Whether a given light chain works this way is character- and game-specific, so check it in training mode.

    Element 20: Earning an Extra Step

    Use the threat of an effective poke to secure an extra step forward, then use that step to set up a throw mixup.

    Logic:

    • Your poke has made them respect your range
    • Because they respect it, they let you walk a little closer without pressing a button
    • From that closer position, you threaten both a throw and a strike
    • They have to guess, and you got there without taking a risk

    Mastery Level: This is pure footsies: you gain ground because of what you could do, not what you did.

    Element 21: Walking Back After a Blocked Jump-In

    After blocking a long-range jump attack, try walking backward instead of low blocking if you expect your opponent to attempt anything fancy.

    Post-Block Options:

    • Walk back: Their follow-up (throw, short combo starter, low) whiffs, and their spacing is ruined
    • Block: Safe default, but you stay in their pressure
    • Counterattack: Possible if there is a real gap, but risky if they expect it

    Advanced: Because the jump-in hit from far away, they usually land at a distance where their follow-ups only just reach. A small step back is often enough to make them whiff.

    Element 22: Spacing Pokes So the Counterpoke Whiffs

    Make your opponent block your poke at the exact distance where their counterpoke will barely whiff from where they end up after blockstun pushback.

    Calculation:

    • Your poke hits from slightly further than their best counterpoke
    • After blocking, pushback moves them to a distance just outside their counterpoke's reach
    • If they press it, it whiffs
    • You whiff punish it

    Result: The poke tempts them to press a button while you remain safe. Learning these exact distances for your main matchups is training-mode homework.

    Elements 23-26: Hopkick Mastery

    Element 23: Safe Hopkick Setups

    A hopkick is a short hopping attack that travels forward and automatically avoids low attacks. It is too quick to counter on reaction, and even when blocked it can gain momentum, meter and position. Hopkicks are practically free after certain moves, at certain ranges, in certain matchups.

    Safe Application:

    • Find the moves, ranges and matchups where your hopkick can't be punished
    • Use it where the opponent likes to press low pokes (it hops over them)
    • Once the opponent fears the hopkick, they play more defensively
    • A defensive opponent opens up throw opportunities

    Why Strong: It beats low pokes and pushes the opponent into predictable defensive responses. It is not a universal tool: it still loses to well-timed standing attacks and anti-airs. In SF6, classic hopkicks are rare, so treat this element as theory for any forward-moving, low-crushing move.

    Element 24: Hopkicks Up Close

    Up close, hopkicks can extend offensive sequences or interrupt telegraphed poke strings.

    Pattern:

    • Use a hopkick after your pressure to continue the offense from a new angle
    • If the opponent has a predictable low poke string, hop over it and hit them
    • Some hopkicks are truly airborne, which makes them temporarily immune to throws

    Opponent's Dilemma: If they keep pressing low buttons, they get hopped over. If they stop, you've regained control of the close-range game.

    Element 25: Divekicks as Hopkicks

    Characters with air divekicks that have no minimum jump height can use them like hopkicks.

    Examples from the handbook:

    • ST Dhalsim's drill (j.D+MK)
    • SF4 Cammy's Cannon Strike
    • Done right after leaving the ground, a low divekick moves forward, avoids lows and comes down fast

    Trade-off: Whether a divekick works this way depends on its minimum height, its advantage on block, and how easy it is to anti-air in that specific game. Check those details for your character in training mode.

    Element 26: Defending Against Hopkicks

    In most cases, preventing opponents from establishing their ideal hopkick range is the wisest solution.

    Defense:

    • Control the range where their hopkick is safe so they can't set it up
    • If they do establish it, the safest response is to block and think about what they will do next
    • Standing jab is worth trying as a quick interrupt (the handbook notes it works against divekicks too)
    • Avoid low pokes in that range: that is exactly what hopkicks beat

    Balance: For the hopkick user, the lesson is the same as always: don't become predictable by relying only on the hopkick.

    Elements 27-30: Jumping in Footsies & Okizeme

    Element 27: Jump After a Knockdown

    Eliminate the opponent's ability to anti-air by knocking them down first, then jump at them as they stand up.

    Positioning Decisions:

    • Knockdown first: Land a sweep (or a well-read DP) so they're on the ground
    • Jump timing: Jump so you arrive as they wake up; a grounded opponent can't anti-air while knocked down
    • Safety: They can still use an invincible reversal on wakeup unless your jump is a properly timed safe jump

    Advanced: Different knockdowns leave different distances and timings. Learning the right jump for each knockdown takes practice.

    Element 28: Jump Over a Whiffed Slow Attack

    Bait your opponent into committing a slow attack and jump over them as it whiffs.

    Scenario:

    • Use tick attacks, fake throws, or the threat of your ground footsies to make them commit
    • They throw out a slow attack (e.g. a sweep or a heavy poke)
    • You jump as it comes out; they're stuck in recovery and can't anti-air
    • You land a jump-in combo

    Mind Game: Because they're focused on the ground game, they're not looking for the jump. That's what makes it work.

    Element 29: Chaos into Crossup

    Do something chaotic, then go for a crossup while the confusion throws off their reflexes.

    Application:

    • Create a hectic moment: a sudden burst of offense, an odd movement, an unexpected button
    • While they're still processing it, jump for the crossup
    • A confused opponent reacts late or chooses the wrong block direction

    Limit: Keep doing it only while the risk/reward is in your favor. Once they stop being confused, the jump becomes a liability.

    Element 30: Stopping Crossups

    Shut down an opponent's crossup attempt with a vertically aimed normal, then time your own jump to catch them.

    Options:

    • Vertical normal: An attack that hits above your head beats the crossup before it lands
    • Air-to-air: Win the air exchange, then jump again immediately
    • Jump over them: Against distant jumpers, jump over them as they come down

    Result: Once their crossup attempts stop working, they have to fight you on the ground, in the footsies game.

    Elements 31-35: Misdirection

    Element 31: Meaningless but Safe Actions

    Do something completely meaningless yet relatively safe, for no reason, so the opponent can't predict your real tactics.

    Examples:

    • At a few key moments, randomly whiff a jab instead of pressing the obvious button
    • Occasionally whiff your best move at a range where it can't be punished

    Purpose: The opponent is reading your patterns. A few safe, random actions blur those patterns so they can't predict your best moves.

    Element 32: Skipping Beats

    Mix up your timing by skipping beats rather than by pressing different buttons.

    Example: Walk into c.MK range but don't press it right away. Hang around that distance for a second to lure your opponent into a false sense of security.

    Mixup:

    • Poke as soon as you reach range → they learn to expect it
    • Reach range and wait → they relax or press a button
    • Then poke, or punish the button they pressed

    Advanced Weapon: Rhythm changes make their defensive timing unreliable and force them to react instead of predict.

    Element 33: Going on an Offensive Tear

    When the opponent expects you to play defensively, suddenly go on an offensive tear.

    Pattern:

    • Play patient footsies until the opponent settles into it
    • Switch to sustained pressure without warning
    • Return to patient play before they adjust

    Key: This depends on surprise, so you need a good sense of match flow before you flip the switch.

    Element 34: Wakeup c.LP

    On paper, few things are less effective than pressing c.LP on wakeup. That is exactly why, used sparingly, it wreaks havoc on cautious players' plans.

    Why it works:

    • A cautious opponent sets up a slow or delayed attack on your wakeup
    • A fast light attack interrupts it
    • Now they doubt their setup and become more hesitant

    Warning: Against a proper meaty, this gets you hit (in SF6, by a Counter or Punish Counter). It's a misdirection tool, not a defensive habit.

    Element 35: Doing Weird, Confusing Things

    When momentum is going against you, do lots of weird, confusing things to break the opponent's rhythm.

    Process:

    • Notice: The opponent is in a groove and you're losing exchanges
    • Disrupt: Do something odd but low-risk (the handbook suggests repeatedly stomping the ground at mid-range)
    • Exploit: If it makes them question themselves even for a split second, that may be the opening you need

    Bigger Picture: The misdirection elements all work the same way: put unpredictability into the natural gaps of your gameplan so the opponent can never fully read you.

    Positioning: Start and Maintain Advantage

    Critical question in each matchup: "What is my ideal zone?"

    Then: "How do I get there? How do I maintain it? How do I escape if they force me out?"

    Scenario Goal Strategy
    Round Start Establish ideal position Walk, dash or back off toward your zone. Start poking.
    Post-Combo Reset to advantageous position Choose combo enders for what they give you: corner carry, knockdown with oki, or the distance you want.
    Post-Throw Maintain advantage after throw Throws give a knockdown. Use the time to move to your oki position, or toward the corner.
    Blocking Jump Don't let them advance too much Stand block the jump attack (jump-ins hit high), anti-air when you can, or walk back (Element 21). Maintain distance.
    Corner Trapped Escape or minimize damage Block patiently and look for a real gap: invincible reversal, jump (including neutral jump), throw tech, Drive Parry or Drive Reversal. Avoid Burnout in the corner: a blocked Drive Impact there stuns you.
