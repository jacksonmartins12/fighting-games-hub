What Are Fighting Games?

Fighting games are not, as commonly believed, "button mashing" simulators. Instead, they are:

  • Speed Chess: Planning multiple moves ahead under pressure
  • Poker: Opponent reading, bluffing, probability calculation
  • Magic The Gathering: Finite resource management (in SF6: Drive Gauge and Super Art gauge)
  • Rock-Paper-Scissors: Decisions with immediate and significant consequences
  • 1v1 Combat: Exploitation of ranges, timings and psychology

Deep Dive: Why These Comparisons?

Each analogy reveals a unique aspect of fighting games:

Chess: You're thinking multiple moves ahead, positioning, and sequences. Each move has long-term consequences. You study openings (the first exchanges of a round), midgame (footsies), and endgame (low-health situations where one more hit or one chip of damage decides the round).

Poker: You have incomplete information. You read opponent tells (patterns, hesitation). You calculate odds (what's the chance he has meter? will he jump? will he throw?). You bluff with moves that appear vulnerable.

MTG: You have resources: meter (in SF6, the Drive Gauge for Drive moves and the Super Art gauge for supers), health (life), position (zone). Each spent resource has a cost. Mismanaging meter means losing critical opportunities.

Why Study Fighting Games?

The question is not trivial. Fighting games offer:

Cognitive Development

Learn pattern recognition, adaptation, problem diagnosis under extreme stress. These skills transfer to any competitive domain.

Sensorimotor Training

Develop precision, timing, and reactivity in fractions of a second. Your body learns to execute complex movements automatically.

Technical Analysis

Learn to investigate systems in detail: pixels, frames, hitboxes, frame advantage. Reverse game design.

Community & Competition

Make genuine connections with other competitors. Learn through friendly rivalry.

Fighting Games as Inverse Game Design

Key Concept: Inverted Game Design

While game designers create systems and try to balance them, you (the advanced player) are doing the opposite:

  • Studying every pixel and frame of the system
  • Finding imbalances and exploits
  • Reducing the game to its basic components
  • Finding the optimal strategies
  • Transforming the game into "a mess of broken parts"

You're essentially telling the designer: "Your game is this, see how you made it".

The Learning Journey: 4 Phases of Mastery

Learning fighting games tends to follow a recognizable pattern. This guide adapts the "Four Stages of Competence" learning model, whose classic order is unconscious incompetence, conscious incompetence, conscious competence and unconscious competence. Here the order is adjusted for fighting games: the plateau is treated as a phase where unconscious incompetence (blind spots you can't see) comes back at a higher level. The durations below are rough illustrations, not measurements; everyone progresses at a different pace.

Phase 1: Incompetent & Conscious (Beginner)

You KNOW you don't know. Everything is new. You're learning fast because every single day brings new discoveries.

Duration: 1-4 weeks | Improvement Rate: Very fast (noticeable progress almost every session)

What's Happening: You're absorbing fundamentals at maximum rate. Attack, block, throw are concepts you're discovering. Your body is learning from zero.

Typical Experience: Beating you is easy. You lose constantly. But you don't mind because everything is new and interesting. You're having fun discovering the game.

Mistake to Avoid: Overthinking. Beginners often research too much (tier lists, optimal combos, pro strategies) instead of just playing. This delays progression.

What to Focus On: The 3 fundamentals (attack, block, throw). Spend 80% of time playing, 20% learning basic concepts.

Phase 2: Competent & Conscious (Intermediate)

You can do things but need to THINK. Execution is hard, decisions are slow. You're no longer a complete beginner, but you're not yet automatic.

Duration: 4-16 weeks | Improvement Rate: Moderate (steady, but slower and less obvious)

What's Happening: You've learned the basics. Now you're combining them. You can do a 2-hit combo if you focus. You can anti-air if you're paying attention. But anything complex breaks under pressure.

Typical Experience: You beat other beginners. You get destroyed by intermediate players. Frustration starts setting in because you can SEE the gap between what you can do and what you need to do, but you can't yet bridge it.

Mistake to Avoid: Trying to learn too many things at once. You want to learn combos, footsies, strategy, all simultaneously. This overwhelms you.

What to Focus On: ONE skill at a time. Master 3 basic combos. THEN learn footsies basics. THEN layer on strategy. Sequential learning > parallel learning at this stage.

Phase 3: Unconscious Incompetence Returns (The Plateau) DANGER ZONE

You think you know, but there are deep gaps in your knowledge. You stop improving despite grinding.

Duration: 2-12 months | Improvement Rate: Stalled (results flat or even getting worse for weeks at a time)

What's Happening: This is the most dangerous phase. You've learned enough to beat casual players consistently. You think you understand footsies, combos, strategy. But you don't. There are HUGE blind spots you can't see because you don't know what you don't know.

Typical Experience: You can beat intermediate players, but consistently lose to advanced players. You blame external factors ("He's just cheap", "My character sucks", "The matchup is bad"). You grind for 2 hours and feel like you're not improving. Frustration peaks.

Why This Happens: You've built habits. Bad habits. You poke the same way every time. You throw from the same distance. You anti-air the same way. Advanced opponents see these patterns and exploit them. But since you're not learning new things (you think you know everything), you can't adapt.

The Wall: Many players quit here. They hit this plateau around month 4-6 and think they've reached their limit. They haven't. They just need to break through.

How to Escape Phase 3:

  • 1) Recognize You're Here: If you've been grinding for 3+ months and your win rate isn't improving, you're in Phase 3. Acknowledge it.
  • 2) Ask for Help: Find better players. Play them. Lose repeatedly. After 20 losses, watch a replay. Ask them: "What am I doing wrong?" This reveals blind spots.
  • 3) Deliberately Practice Weakness: When better players beat you the same way 5x in a row, THAT'S the weakness to lab. Not your comfortable combos.
  • 4) Change Your Approach: Stop grinding casual matches. Instead, do targeted practice: 30 minutes anti-air drills, 30 minutes throw setups, 30 minutes footsies against human opponent. Specific > general.
  • 5) Document Everything: Keep a practice journal. After each session, write down what you discovered. Over 2-3 weeks, patterns emerge about your weaknesses.

Phase 4: Competent & Unconscious (Advanced) THE GOAL

You do EVERYTHING automatically. Your body knows, your mind is free. You're not thinking about execution or basic strategy. You're thinking 2-3 moves ahead.

Duration: 12+ months | Improvement Rate: Continuous (slower, smaller gains that keep coming)

What's Happening: Combos come out without thinking. Spacing is automatic. Anti-air is reflexive. Your conscious mind is freed up for high-level strategy: reading opponent, adaptation, game theory.

Typical Experience: You beat most intermediate players easily. You can hang with advanced players. You understand matchups at a deep level. You can explain WHY certain strategies work. You adapt mid-match and change your entire gameplan based on opponent patterns.

Characteristic Skill: "The Zone" — moments where time seems to slow down. You can feel what opponent will do before they do it. You react and counter automatically. These moments happen more frequently as you advance.

Never-Ending Improvement: Phase 4 doesn't have an endpoint. Even pro players stay in Phase 4 and keep improving. The ceiling is very high (possibly infinite).

Most players get stuck in Phase 3 (the plateau)

The secret is recognizing when you've stopped improving and deliberately changing your approach. If you're grinding for hours and not seeing results, you're not in the wrong game—you're using the wrong practice method. The Path of Mastery section in Progression and the Deliberate Practice section in Training Regimens show how to deal with plateaus.

Getting Started: Your First Steps

The Most Important Thing: Actually Play

Use whatever controller you already have; don't over-prepare your gear. Avoid the trap of overthinking: researching tier lists, comparing controllers, watching videos about optimal play — these can delay your actual start indefinitely. Instead, pick a game you think is cool and start playing immediately.

Your First Session Strategy

Goal: Have fun. That's it. You're not trying to be optimal.

  • Pick a game that excites you (not what's "best")
  • Select 2-3 characters that interest you
  • Play against CPU in Arcade Mode for 1-2 hours
  • Don't worry about losing — you will lose repeatedly and that's correct
  • Notice moments of joy: cool combos, unexpected wins, satisfying knockdowns
  • Take notes on questions that arise during play

After Your First Session

Keep a notebook (physical or digital) where you jot down:

  • Questions that came up ("Why did I get hit by that?" "How did they do that move?")
  • Moments that felt good or cool
  • Characters or mechanics you want to explore next
  • Frustrations (so you can address them systematically)

Don't overthink this. It's not about perfect analysis — it's about practicing your ability to think and reflect separately from playing. This builds one of fighting games' most valuable skills: the capacity to pause, analyze, and improve.

Your Setup: Consistency Over Expense

Don't let gear delay your start. Once you play regularly, though, your setup starts to matter: timing windows are measured in frames (1/60 of a second), so extra delay from your screen or connection changes your timing.

  • Screen: TVs are often slower than gaming monitors. Turn on your TV's game mode or use a low-latency monitor. If you build your timing on a slow screen and then play on a fast one (or the reverse), you'll have to relearn it.
  • Connection: play online on a cable, not Wi-Fi. Wi-Fi adds delay and instability for you and your opponent.
  • Consistency: whenever you can, practice on the same kind of setup you'll compete on. Habits built under lag, like moves that only work because the opponent can't react in time, fall apart against stronger players.
  • Controller: pad, stick or leverless, pick what's comfortable. Sticks make some things easier (reaching every button, quick double taps, 360 motions) but take time to learn, and offline your opponent can see and hear what you press. No controller makes you good on its own.
