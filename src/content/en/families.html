Fighting Game Families: What Changes From Game to Game

FamiliesAn overview of how other fighting games work, written for SF6 players. Systems in other games also change with patches and sequels, so confirm details in each game's own training mode or wiki.

Most of this guide is about things every fighting game shares: space, frames, risk, reads, practice and nerves. SF6 is the game we cover in depth, so it supplies the examples. This chapter maps the rest of the genre, so that what you learn here carries over when you pick up another game, and so you know which habits to bring and which to leave behind.

What Every Game Shares

Strip away the systems and every competitive fighting game asks the same questions. Who controls the space between the characters? Whose turn is it, and by how many frames? What does each option risk and what does it pay? What will this opponent do next? The answers change from game to game; the questions don't.

Traditional 2D: Street Fighter, King of Fighters, Samurai Shodown

The family SF6 belongs to. Fights happen mostly on the ground, a jump is a commitment that can be anti-aired, and projectiles control space. Specials use motion or charge inputs, and the high, low and overhead mixup decides how you block.

The King of Fighters: More Ways to Move

KOF keeps the 2D basics but adds movement and team play. Four buttons (A, B, C, D for light punch, light kick, heavy punch, heavy kick) and four jump heights: tap up for a short hop, hold it for a full jump, and add a quick down input first for the hyper versions. Hops are fast and low, so the anti-air game is very different from SF6, where jumps are slower and easier to read.

  • Rolls (A+B) pass through attacks but lose to throws.
  • Guard cancels spend one bar to get out of blockstun, either with a roll or with a blowback attack (C+D), much like a Drive Reversal in SF6.
  • Teams of three fight one after another, so you choose an order and manage resources across characters.

Samurai Shodown: The Opposite Extreme

Weapon-based, with few hits and huge damage per hit. A single heavy slash can take a large share of the life bar, so whiff punishing and patience matter even more than in SF6, and combos matter much less. It's a good reminder that footsies and risk/reward are the core of the genre, not combos.

Anime and Airdashers: Guilty Gear, BlazBlue, Granblue

Faster games with more movement and more offense. Characters can airdash and usually double jump, so the air is a place to fight, not just a way to approach. Normals chain into each other freely (gatlings), and the attacker gets many more ways to open the defender up.

Guilty Gear Strive as an Example

  • Tension gauge: fills when you move forward and attack, and pays for Overdrives (supers) and Roman Cancels, which cut a move's recovery short to extend combos, make unsafe moves safe or escape pressure.
  • Burst gauge: a separate resource for defensive tools. The Psych Burst blows the opponent away even in the middle of their combo, something SF6 doesn't have.
  • RISC gauge: rises while you block; the higher it is, the more damage you take when you're finally hit. Blocking forever is punished by design.
  • Wall Break: continued pressure at the edge breaks the wall and moves the fight to a new area, with a temporary bonus for the attacker. The corner doesn't last forever the way it does in SF6.
  • Defensive blocking: Faultless Defense spends Tension to block with more pushback, and Instant Block rewards blocking at the last moment.

The lesson for an SF6 player: in airdashers, defense uses resources actively, and the game expects you to spend them. Sitting and blocking is weaker than in SF6.

Tag and Team Games: Marvel, Dragon Ball FighterZ

You control a team of two or three and can call partners in. That changes the math: with an assist, you can attack twice at once, the "attacking by fire" idea from Playing to Win. A projectile plus an assist plus your character's own pressure creates situations one character alone can't.

What Changes in Team Games

  • Assists: a partner comes in for one attack and leaves. They cover your unsafe moves, extend combos and lock the opponent down.
  • Switching and team supers: you can tag in another character, often in the middle of a combo, at the cost of meter.
  • Snowball effect: when a character dies, the next one comes in under pressure. Comebacks and blowouts are both common.
  • Universal approach tools: in Dragon Ball FighterZ, everyone has a Super Dash (a homing forward dash), a Vanish that teleports behind the opponent for 1 Ki gauge, and a Dragon Rush that the opponent must tech instead of block.

3D and Platform Fighters

3D fighters (Tekken, Virtua Fighter, Soulcalibur, Dead or Alive) add a third axis of movement and change how blocking and punishing work. They have their own chapter: 3D Fighters.

Platform fighters (Super Smash Bros. and similar games) are a separate family: damage builds a percentage instead of draining a life bar, you win by knocking the opponent off the stage, and stages have platforms and ledges. Spacing, whiff punishing and reads still apply, but the systems are different enough that this guide doesn't cover them.

Reading Other Notations

The numpad notation used in this guide (236P, 2MK) is the standard for most 2D games. What changes is the button names after the number:

  • Guilty Gear and most anime games: P, K, S (slash), H (heavy slash), D (dust). 5H is standing heavy slash, 2D is a crouching dust (a sweep).
  • King of Fighters: A, B, C, D (light punch, light kick, heavy punch, heavy kick). 2B is a crouching light kick.
  • Dragon Ball FighterZ: L, M, H, S (light, medium, heavy, special).
  • Tekken: numbers are buttons, not directions: 1 and 2 are left and right punch, 3 and 4 are left and right kick. Directions are letters: d/f+2 is down-forward plus right punch, and WS means "while standing up from a crouch".
  • Virtua Fighter: P, K and G (guard) with arrows for directions.
  • Soulcalibur: A (horizontal attack), B (vertical attack), K (kick), G (guard).

Learning a New Game: The First Questions

When you pick up any new fighting game, answer these questions before worrying about combos. They map onto the chapters of The Path, so you already know what each one is for.

Ten Questions for Any New Game

  1. How do I block, and which attacks hit high, low or overhead?
  2. What is my fastest normal, and how minus does a move have to be before I can punish it?
  3. How do throws and throw escapes work?
  4. What are my universal escape options: invincible moves, parries, bursts, rolls, sidesteps?
  5. What do the meters do, how do they fill, and which ones carry over between rounds?
  6. How do I stop jumps or other aerial approaches?
  7. What are my options when I'm knocked down?
  8. What happens at the edge of the stage: a corner, a wall or a ring out?
  9. How is the notation written, and what do the community's terms mean?
  10. Where is the community wiki? SuperCombo covers Street Fighter, Dustloop covers Guilty Gear, BlazBlue and Dragon Ball FighterZ, Dream Cancel covers KOF, and Tekken players use Wavu Wiki and TekkenDocs.

Your SF6 Hours Are Not Wasted

Players who switch games bring their fundamentals with them. The first weeks in a new game are about relearning numbers and systems; the months of spacing, defense, reads and mental game you built in SF6 come along. That's the reason this guide keeps the universal ideas apart from the SF6 module.
